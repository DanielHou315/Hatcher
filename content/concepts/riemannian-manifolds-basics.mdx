## Why you need this

Flow matching moves a sample along $\dot x_t = v_\theta(t,x_t)$ and trains $v_\theta$ to match
$x_1 - x_0$, the velocity of the straight line from noise to data. On the set of rotations there
are no straight lines, and $R_1 - R_0$ is not a tangent vector. Riemannian flow matching (Chen
and Lipman, <Cite id="2302.03660" />) replaces the line by a geodesic, the difference by a
logarithm map, and the Euclidean norm in the loss by a Riemannian metric; its main theorem is
the Euclidean one with the continuity equation rewritten on the manifold. Diffusion and flow
models for protein backbones (FrameDiff <Cite id="2302.02277" />, FoldFlow
<Cite id="2310.02391" />) and for robot grasps and end-effector poses (SE(3)-DiffusionFields
<Cite id="2209.03855" />) do exactly this on $SO(3)$ and $SE(3)$, while Diffusion Policy
(<Cite id="2303.04137" />) avoids it by treating a 6-D rotation encoding as Euclidean. To read
either kind of paper, or to decide which to build, you need the five objects on this page:
manifold, tangent space, metric, geodesic with its $\exp$ and $\log$, and volume form.

## Setup

We only need manifolds that sit inside $\mathbb{R}^n$ and are cut out by equations; this covers
spheres, tori, $SO(3)$ and $SE(3)$, and avoids charts and atlases entirely.

<Definition name="Embedded manifold and tangent space">
Let $F:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^k$ be smooth and suppose the Jacobian $DF(x)$ has full rank
$k$ at every $x$ with $F(x)=0$. Then $M = \{x\in\mathbb{R}^n : F(x) = 0\}$ is an embedded
manifold of dimension $d = n-k$, and its tangent space at $x\in M$ is

$$
T_xM = \ker DF(x) = \{ v\in\mathbb{R}^n : DF(x)\,v = 0\},
$$

a $d$-dimensional linear subspace. Equivalently, $T_xM$ is the set of velocities $\dot\gamma(0)$
of smooth curves $\gamma$ in $M$ with $\gamma(0) = x$: differentiate $F(\gamma(s)) = 0$.
</Definition>

Two examples carry the whole page. The sphere $S^2 = \{x\in\mathbb{R}^3 : \|x\|^2 = 1\}$ has
$F(x) = \|x\|^2 - 1$, $DF(x) = 2x^\top$, so $T_xS^2 = \{v : \langle x, v\rangle = 0\}$. The
rotation group $SO(3)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$ has $F(R) = R^\top R - I$ (six independent
equations), $DF(R)V = R^\top V + V^\top R$, and $T_RSO(3) = \{R\,\omega^\wedge : \omega\in\mathbb{R}^3\}$,
which is the [Lie algebra](/concepts/lie-algebra-and-perturbations) translated to $R$.

<Definition name="Riemannian metric, length, distance">
A Riemannian metric assigns to each $x\in M$ an inner product $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_x$ on
$T_xM$, varying smoothly with $x$. The length of a curve $\gamma:[a,b]\to M$ is
$\int_a^b\|\dot\gamma(t)\|_{\gamma(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t$, and the distance $d(x,y)$ is the infimum
of lengths of curves from $x$ to $y$. On $S^2$ we use the metric inherited from $\mathbb{R}^3$,
$\langle u,v\rangle_x = u^\top v$. On $SO(3)$ we use
$\langle R\omega^\wedge, R\eta^\wedge\rangle_R = \omega^\top\eta$, which equals half the
Frobenius inner product and is bi-invariant: left or right multiplication by a fixed rotation
preserves it.
</Definition>

The gradient of $f:M\to\mathbb{R}$ is the tangent vector $\nabla_g f(x)\in T_xM$ with
$\langle\nabla_g f, v\rangle_x = \mathrm{d}f(x)[v]$ for all tangent $v$; for the inherited
metric on an embedded manifold it is the orthogonal projection of the Euclidean gradient onto
$T_xM$. On $S^2$, $\nabla_g f(x) = (I - xx^\top)\nabla f(x)$.

## The idea

<Definition name="Geodesic, exponential map, logarithm map">
A geodesic is a curve $\gamma$ in $M$ with constant speed whose acceleration is orthogonal to
the manifold: $\ddot\gamma(t)\perp T_{\gamma(t)}M$ for all $t$ (this is the embedded-manifold
form of the geodesic equation; Lee Ch. 5). Geodesics locally minimize length, and through each
$x$ with each initial velocity $v\in T_xM$ there is exactly one, $\gamma_{x,v}$, by ODE
uniqueness. The exponential map and its local inverse are

$$
\exp_x(v) = \gamma_{x,v}(1),
\qquad
\log_x(y) = \text{the } v\in T_xM \text{ of smallest norm with } \exp_x(v) = y ,
$$

and then $d(x,y) = \|\log_x(y)\|_x$ whenever the minimizing geodesic is unique. The path
$t\mapsto\exp_x(t\log_x(y))$, $t\in[0,1]$, is the geodesic from $x$ to $y$ traversed at
constant speed $d(x,y)$.
</Definition>

<Theorem name="Geodesics of the sphere" label="Thm 1">
On $S^2$ with the inherited metric, for $x\in S^2$ and $v\in T_xS^2$ with $\|v\| = r > 0$,

$$
\exp_x(v) = \cos r\,x + \sin r\,\frac{v}{r},
\qquad
\log_x(y) = \frac{\theta}{\sin\theta}\big(y - \cos\theta\,x\big),\ \ \theta = \arccos\langle x,y\rangle\in(0,\pi),
$$

and $d(x,y) = \theta$. Geodesics are great circles, and $\log_x$ is defined for all $y\neq -x$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Let $\gamma(t) = \cos(tr)\,x + \sin(tr)\,v/r$. Since $x\perp v$ and both $x$ and $v/r$ are
unit vectors, $\|\gamma(t)\|^2 = \cos^2(tr) + \sin^2(tr) = 1$, so $\gamma$ lies in $S^2$.
Differentiate: $\dot\gamma = r\big(-\sin(tr)\,x + \cos(tr)\,v/r\big)$ has constant norm $r$,
and $\ddot\gamma = -r^2\gamma$, which is a multiple of the position vector $\gamma(t)$, hence
orthogonal to $T_{\gamma(t)}S^2$. So $\gamma$ is a geodesic with $\gamma(0) = x$,
$\dot\gamma(0) = v$; by uniqueness $\exp_x(v) = \gamma(1)$. It traces the great circle in the
plane spanned by $x$ and $v$.

For the logarithm, given $y\neq\pm x$ let $\theta = \arccos\langle x,y\rangle$ and
$w = y - \langle x,y\rangle x = y - \cos\theta\,x$, the component of $y$ orthogonal to $x$;
$\|w\|^2 = 1 - \cos^2\theta = \sin^2\theta$. Put $v = \theta\,w/\sin\theta$, so $\|v\| = \theta$
and $\exp_x(v) = \cos\theta\,x + \sin\theta\,w/\sin\theta = \cos\theta\,x + w = y$. Any other
preimage has norm $2\pi k\pm\theta$ with $k\ge1$, larger than $\theta$, so this is
$\log_x(y)$, and $d(x,y) = \theta$ because great-circle arcs shorter than $\pi$ minimize length
(Lee Thm 6.4 gives the general statement; for the sphere it also follows from the spherical
triangle inequality). $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Proposition name="Geodesics of SO(3)" label="Prop 2">
With the bi-invariant metric, the geodesic through $R$ with velocity $R\omega^\wedge$ is
$\gamma(t) = R\exp(t\omega^\wedge)$. Hence

$$
\exp_R(R\omega^\wedge) = R\exp(\omega^\wedge),
\qquad
\log_R(R') = R\,\big(\operatorname{Log}(R^\top R')\big)^\wedge,
\qquad
d(R,R') = \big\|\operatorname{Log}(R^\top R')\big\| ,
$$

so the Riemannian $\exp$ and $\log$ are the group exponential and logarithm of the
[previous pages](/concepts/lie-groups-so3-se3), and the distance is the angle of the relative
rotation.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
$\ddot\gamma(t) = R\exp(t\omega^\wedge)(\omega^\wedge)^2 = \gamma(t)\,(\omega^\wedge)^2$. The
tangent space at $\gamma(t)$ is $\gamma(t)\cdot\{\text{skew}\}$, and $(\omega^\wedge)^2$ is
symmetric. Under the Frobenius inner product a symmetric matrix is orthogonal to every skew
matrix ($\operatorname{tr}(S^\top A) = \operatorname{tr}(SA) = -\operatorname{tr}(AS)$ forces
it to vanish), and left multiplication by $\gamma(t)$ preserves the Frobenius product. So the
acceleration is normal to the manifold, and the speed $\|\omega\|$ is constant. The formulas
follow by evaluating at $t = 1$ and inverting. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

Densities on $M$ need a reference measure, the analogue of Lebesgue measure.

<Definition name="Riemannian volume">
Let $\varphi:U\subset\mathbb{R}^d\to M$ be a smooth parameterization (a chart) and
$G(u)_{ij} = \langle\partial_i\varphi(u),\partial_j\varphi(u)\rangle_{\varphi(u)}$ its metric
matrix. The Riemannian volume measure is $\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_g = \sqrt{\det G(u)}\,\mathrm{d}u$
on the image of the chart; it does not depend on the chart, by the change of variables
formula. A density on $M$ is a function $p\ge0$ with $\int_M p\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_g = 1$.
The divergence of a tangent vector field $u$ is
$\operatorname{div}_g u = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\det G}}\partial_i\big(\sqrt{\det G}\,u^i\big)$ in
chart coordinates, the unique operator with
$\int_M\langle u,\nabla_g f\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_g = -\int_M f\,\operatorname{div}_g u\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_g$
for compactly supported $f$.
</Definition>

With these, the [continuity equation](/concepts/continuity-equation) keeps its form: if
$x_t$ follows $\dot x_t = u_t(x_t)$ on $M$ with $x_0\sim p_0$, then the density of $x_t$ with
respect to $\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_g$ satisfies $\partial_tp_t + \operatorname{div}_g(p_tu_t) = 0$.
The proof is the Euclidean one: differentiate $\int f(x_t)$ for a test function $f$ and integrate
by parts with $\operatorname{div}_g$.

<Theorem name="Riemannian conditional flow matching, simple geometries" label="Thm 3">
(Chen and Lipman <Cite id="2302.03660" />.) Let $M$ have closed-form $\exp$ and
$\log$, let $q$ be the data distribution on $M$ and $p_0$ a noise distribution. For
$x_0\sim p_0$, $x_1\sim q$ independent, define the geodesic conditional path and its velocity

$$
x_t = \exp_{x_1}\!\big((1-t)\log_{x_1}(x_0)\big),
\qquad
u_t(x_t\mid x_1) = \frac{\mathrm{d}x_t}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\log_{x_t}(x_1)}{1-t},
$$

so $\|u_t\|_{x_t} = d(x_0,x_1)$ for all $t$. Let $p_t$ be the marginal law of $x_t$ and
$u_t(x) = \mathbb{E}[u_t(x_t\mid x_1)\mid x_t = x]$. Then $u_t$ is a tangent vector field
whose flow transports $p_0$ to $p_1 = q$, and the losses

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{RFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,x_t}\big\|v_\theta(t,x_t) - u_t(x_t)\big\|^2_{x_t},
\qquad
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{RCFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,x_0,x_1}\big\|v_\theta(t,x_t) - u_t(x_t\mid x_1)\big\|^2_{x_t}
$$

have the same gradient in $\theta$, for $v_\theta(t,x)\in T_xM$. Sampling integrates
$\dot x = v_\theta(t,x)$, for instance by $x\leftarrow\exp_x\big(h\,v_\theta(t,x)\big)$.
</Theorem>

The proof is the one on the [flow matching](/concepts/flow-matching) page with two
substitutions: the continuity equation is the Riemannian one above, and the expansion of the
squared norm uses $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{x_t}$, whose cross term
$\mathbb{E}\langle v_\theta, u_t(x_t\mid x_1)\rangle_{x_t}$ equals
$\mathbb{E}\langle v_\theta, u_t(x_t)\rangle_{x_t}$ by the tower property, exactly as in
$\mathbb{R}^d$. The formula $u_t = \log_{x_t}(x_1)/(1-t)$ holds because $x_t$ moves along the
geodesic from $x_0$ to $x_1$ at constant speed $d(x_0,x_1)$, and the remaining arc from $x_t$
to $x_1$ has length $(1-t)\,d(x_0,x_1)$, so $\log_{x_t}(x_1)$ is the velocity scaled by $1-t$.
For manifolds without closed-form geodesics (meshes, for instance) the paper replaces
$\log_{x_1}$ by the gradient of a premetric such as a spectral distance; we do not cover that
case.

<Warning>
On $SO(3)$ the geodesic path is $R_t = R_1\exp\big((1-t)\operatorname{Log}(R_1^\top R_0)\big)$
and the target velocity, expressed as a vector in the body frame of $R_t$, is
$\operatorname{Log}(R_t^\top R_1)/(1-t)$. A network that outputs this vector must have its
output interpreted as $R_t\,v^\wedge\in T_{R_t}SO(3)$, and the loss norm must be the
Riemannian one, i.e. the ordinary Euclidean norm of the $\mathbb{R}^3$ vector, not the
Frobenius norm of $R_tv^\wedge - R_tu^\wedge$ (which is $\sqrt2$ times larger, harmless) and
not the norm of $R_t - R_1$ (which is not a velocity at all).
</Warning>

## Worked example by hand

<Example title="A geodesic flow-matching path on the sphere">
Take $x_1 = (0,0,1)$ (data, the north pole) and $x_0 = (1,0,0)$ (noise, on the equator).
Then $\langle x_0,x_1\rangle = 0$, so $\theta = \pi/2$ and by Thm 1
$\log_{x_1}(x_0) = \frac{\pi/2}{1}\big(x_0 - 0\cdot x_1\big) = (\pi/2, 0, 0)$, tangent at the
pole as it should be. The conditional path is

$$
x_t = \exp_{x_1}\!\big((1-t)(\tfrac{\pi}{2},0,0)\big)
= \cos\!\big((1-t)\tfrac{\pi}{2}\big)\,x_1 + \sin\!\big((1-t)\tfrac{\pi}{2}\big)\,e_1
= \Big(\sin\tfrac{(1-t)\pi}{2},\ 0,\ \cos\tfrac{(1-t)\pi}{2}\Big).
$$

At $t = 0$ this is $(1,0,0) = x_0$, at $t = 1$ it is $(0,0,1) = x_1$. At $t = \tfrac12$,
$x_{1/2} = (\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, 0, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}) = (0.7071, 0, 0.7071)$.

Velocity by differentiating: $\dot x_t = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\big(-\cos\tfrac{(1-t)\pi}{2}, 0, \sin\tfrac{(1-t)\pi}{2}\big)$,
so $\dot x_{1/2} = \tfrac{\pi}{2}(-0.7071, 0, 0.7071) = (-1.1107, 0, 1.1107)$. Checks:
$\langle\dot x_{1/2}, x_{1/2}\rangle = -0.7854 + 0.7854 = 0$, tangent; and
$\|\dot x_{1/2}\| = \pi/2 = d(x_0,x_1)$, constant speed.

Velocity by the formula $\log_{x_t}(x_1)/(1-t)$: $\langle x_{1/2}, x_1\rangle = 0.7071$, so
$\theta' = \pi/4 = (1-t)\,\pi/2$, consistent. Then

$$
\log_{x_{1/2}}(x_1) = \frac{\pi/4}{\sin(\pi/4)}\big(x_1 - \cos(\pi/4)\,x_{1/2}\big)
= \frac{\pi}{4}\cdot\sqrt2\cdot\big(-\tfrac12, 0, \tfrac12\big) = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\big(-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, 0, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big),
$$

and dividing by $1-t = \tfrac12$ gives $\tfrac{\pi}{2}(-0.7071, 0, 0.7071)$, the same vector.

One sampler step. Starting at $x_0$ with the true field $u_0(x_0\mid x_1) = \log_{x_0}(x_1) = (0,0,\pi/2)$
and step $h = \tfrac12$: $\exp_{x_0}\big(\tfrac12(0,0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})\big) = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{4}\,x_0 + \sin\tfrac{\pi}{4}\,e_3 = (0.7071, 0, 0.7071) = x_{1/2}$,
exactly on the path, because a geodesic step along a geodesic path makes no error. The
Euclidean step $x_0 + h\,u_0 = (1, 0, 0.7854)$ has norm $1.27$ and is not on the sphere; a
projection would land it at $(0.786, 0, 0.618)$, off the geodesic and $6.9^\circ$ short of $x_{1/2}$.
</Example>

<Example title="Volume on the sphere and a wrapped Gaussian">
Parameterize $S^2$ by $\varphi(\phi,\psi) = (\sin\phi\cos\psi, \sin\phi\sin\psi, \cos\phi)$
with $\phi\in(0,\pi)$ the polar angle. Then
$\partial_\phi\varphi = (\cos\phi\cos\psi, \cos\phi\sin\psi, -\sin\phi)$ and
$\partial_\psi\varphi = (-\sin\phi\sin\psi, \sin\phi\cos\psi, 0)$, so
$G = \operatorname{diag}(1, \sin^2\phi)$ and $\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_g = \sin\phi\,\mathrm{d}\phi\,\mathrm{d}\psi$.
The uniform density is $p = 1/(4\pi)$, and indeed
$\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^\pi\frac{\sin\phi}{4\pi}\,\mathrm{d}\phi\,\mathrm{d}\psi = \frac{2\cdot2\pi}{4\pi} = 1$.

Now the manifold analogue of a Gaussian centered at the north pole $x$: draw
$v\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2I_2)$ in $T_xS^2$ and set $y = \exp_x(v)$ (a wrapped normal). In
polar coordinates $(r,\alpha)$ on the tangent plane, $\exp_x$ sends $(r,\alpha)$ to
$(\phi,\psi) = (r,\alpha)$ for $r < \pi$, by Thm 1. Lebesgue measure on the plane is
$r\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}\alpha$ while $\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_g = \sin r\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}\alpha$,
so the [change of variables](/concepts/gaussian-computations) gives, for $d(x,y) = r < \pi$
and ignoring wrap-around at $\sigma\ll\pi$,

$$
p(y) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}\,e^{-r^2/(2\sigma^2)}\cdot\frac{r}{\sin r}.
$$

Sanity checks: as $r\to0$ the factor $r/\sin r\to1$ and $p$ is the flat Gaussian; at
$r = \pi/2$ the factor is $\pi/2 = 1.571$, so the density per unit area is $57\%$ higher than a
flat Gaussian would say, because $\exp_x$ compresses area near the equator. The same
computation on $SO(3)$ with $v\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2I_3)$ in the Lie algebra gives the factor
$1/\det J_r(v) = \theta^2/(2(1-\cos\theta))$ with $\theta = \|v\|$, since $\det J_r$ has
eigenvalue $1$ along $v$ and determinant $(\sin\theta/\theta)^2 + ((1-\cos\theta)/\theta)^2 = 2(1-\cos\theta)/\theta^2$
on the orthogonal plane.
</Example>

## Where it shows up

- **Riemannian flow matching** (<Cite id="2302.03660" />). the conditional
  path is written $x_t = \exp_{x_1}(\kappa(t)\log_{x_1}(x_0))$ with a decreasing scheduler
  $\kappa$, $\kappa(t) = 1-t$ here; the marginal-field theorem is stated with the
  Riemannian continuity equation; the experiments on spheres, tori and $SO(3)$ use exactly the
  $\exp$ and $\log$ of Thm 1 and Prop 2. FoldFlow (<Cite id="2310.02391" />) is this recipe on
  $SE(3)^N$ for protein frames, with the $SO(3)$ path
  $R_t = R_0\exp\big(t\operatorname{Log}(R_0^\top R_1)\big)$ and loss on the tangent vector.
- **Diffusion on $SO(3)$.** FrameDiff (<Cite id="2302.02277" />) needs a forward noising
  process on rotations. The Euclidean Gaussian is replaced by the heat kernel on $SO(3)$, the
  isotropic Gaussian $\mathrm{IG}_{SO(3)}(\sigma)$, whose density with respect to the Haar
  volume depends only on the angle $\theta$: $f_\sigma(\theta) = \sum_{l\ge0}(2l+1)\,e^{-l(l+1)\sigma^2/2}\,\sin((l+\tfrac12)\theta)/\sin(\theta/2)$.
  As $\sigma\to\infty$ only $l=0$ survives and $f\to1$, the uniform (Haar) distribution, whose
  angle marginal is $(1-\cos\theta)/\pi$ by the volume computation of the last example. The
  score is then a Riemannian gradient in $T_RSO(3)$, and the reverse SDE steps with
  $\exp_R$, i.e. $R\leftarrow R\exp(\cdot^\wedge)$.
- **Policies that output rotations.** SE(3)-DiffusionFields (<Cite id="2209.03855" />) puts
  Langevin dynamics on grasp poses in $SE(3)$ with the noise in $\mathfrak{se}(3)$ and the
  score as a tangent vector; Diffusion-EDFs (Ryu et al. 2023) do the same with an equivariant
  score. Diffusion Policy (<Cite id="2303.04137" />) instead predicts positions plus the 6-D
  rotation encoding (first two columns of $R$) and adds Euclidean noise in $\mathbb{R}^6$. That
  is legitimate as a generative model on $\mathbb{R}^6$, but the intermediate $x_t$ are not
  rotations, the network's denoising target $x_1 - x_0$ is not a tangent vector, and the
  Gram–Schmidt projection at the end is not $\exp$: as the last example shows, it lands off the
  geodesic. Mean predictions in a chart are also biased: the Euclidean average of the
  rotations by $\pm90^\circ$ about $z$ is $\operatorname{diag}(0,0,1)$, not a rotation at all,
  whereas the Riemannian mean (Fréchet mean, the minimizer of $\sum d(R,R_i)^2$) is $I$. Which
  design wins empirically is an open question; understanding the difference is not.
- **Uncertainty on poses.** The concentrated Gaussian $R = \bar R\exp(\varepsilon^\wedge)$ of the
  [previous page](/concepts/lie-algebra-and-perturbations) is the wrapped normal above on
  $SO(3)$; its normalizing constant and its density with respect to Haar carry the
  $\det J_r$ factor computed there.

## Exercises

<Exercise hint="Write the two rotations with Rodrigues, average the matrices, and compute the Frobenius distance to I and to the projected (Gram-Schmidt) matrix; then compute Log of each and average in the algebra.">
Let $R_\pm = \exp((\pm\tfrac{\pi}{2}e_3)^\wedge)$. Compute the Euclidean average
$\tfrac12(R_+ + R_-)$ and show it is singular. Then show that $I$ minimizes
$d(R,R_+)^2 + d(R,R_-)^2$ over rotations about $e_3$ by writing $R = \exp((\alpha e_3)^\wedge)$
and minimizing over $\alpha$.
</Exercise>

<Exercise hint="Use Thm 1 with x = e_3 and check that exp_x(log_x(y)) = y; the angle is 60 degrees.">
Let $x = (0,0,1)$ and $y = (\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}, 0, \tfrac12)$. Compute $\log_x(y)$, $d(x,y)$,
the midpoint $\exp_x(\tfrac12\log_x(y))$, and verify the midpoint is equidistant from $x$ and $y$.
</Exercise>

<Exercise hint="Differentiate the geodesic R_1 exp((1-t) w^) with w = Log(R_1^T R_0), and use Prop 2's log formula on R_t^T R_1 = exp(-(1-t) w^).">
On $SO(3)$, verify directly that the path $R_t = R_1\exp\big((1-t)\operatorname{Log}(R_1^\top R_0)\big)$
satisfies $\dot R_t = R_t\,u_t^\wedge$ with $u_t = \operatorname{Log}(R_t^\top R_1)/(1-t)$, and
that $\|u_t\| = d(R_0,R_1)$ for all $t$.
</Exercise>

<Exercise hint="The angle marginal of Haar is (1 - cos theta)/pi; integrate f_sigma against it term by term using orthogonality of sin((l+1/2)theta) sin(theta/2) on [0, pi].">
Show that $\int_0^\pi f_\sigma(\theta)\,\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\pi}\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 1$ for
every $\sigma$, so that $\mathrm{IG}_{SO(3)}(\sigma)$ is a probability density with respect to
Haar measure. Which single term of the series contributes?
</Exercise>

## Going deeper

Lee, *Introduction to Riemannian Manifolds*, Ch. 2 (metrics), 5 (geodesics and the exponential
map) and 6 (distance and minimizing curves) is the standard reference and is readable with
this page as a map; Ch. 2 also defines the volume form and divergence properly. Chen and
Lipman (<Cite id="2302.03660" />) is short and should now read as an application: Thm 3 is its
simple-geometry case, and the general case handles geometries without closed-form geodesics
through premetrics and spectral distances. Yim et al. (<Cite id="2302.02277" />) Appendix on
$\mathrm{IG}_{SO(3)}$ is the cleanest derivation of the heat kernel on rotations in the ML
literature.
