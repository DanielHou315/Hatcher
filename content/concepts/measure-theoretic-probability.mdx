## Why you need this

Papers on diffusion models and Schrödinger bridges use four phrases from measure theory as if
they were obvious: a process is "adapted to the filtration $\mathcal F_t$", the Itô integral
is "the $L^2$ limit" of Riemann-type sums, two path measures satisfy "$\mathbb{Q}\ll\mathbb{P}$
with density $\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}/\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}$" (this is what the KL between
diffusions in De Bortoli et al. 2021 means, and what Girsanov's theorem computes), and a
gradient is moved inside an expectation "by dominated convergence" (the policy gradient theorem
and every ELBO gradient). You do not need to be able to construct Lebesgue measure to read those
papers. You need to know what the objects are, which three theorems let you swap limits with
expectations, and why a density is a derivative of one measure with respect to another.

## Setup

A probability space is a triple $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb{P})$: a set of outcomes, a
collection $\mathcal F$ of subsets of $\Omega$ called events, and a probability measure
$\mathbb{P}$ on $\mathcal F$. The point of this page is that $\mathcal F$ is not "all subsets"
and that it carries meaning.

<Definition name="Sigma-algebra, measure, generated sigma-algebra">
A $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal F$ on $\Omega$ is a collection of subsets containing $\Omega$ and
closed under complements and countable unions. A measure $\mu$ on $\mathcal F$ assigns
$\mu(A)\in[0,\infty]$ to each $A\in\mathcal F$ with $\mu(\emptyset) = 0$ and countable
additivity: $\mu(\bigcup_n A_n) = \sum_n\mu(A_n)$ for disjoint $A_n$. A probability measure has
$\mu(\Omega) = 1$. The $\sigma$-algebra generated by a collection $\mathcal C$ of sets,
$\sigma(\mathcal C)$, is the smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing $\mathcal C$. The Borel
$\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal B(\mathbb{R}^d)$ is generated by the open sets; Lebesgue measure
$\lambda$ is the measure on it with $\lambda([a,b]) = b - a$ (in $d=1$), whose existence we take
as known.
</Definition>

<Intuition>
Read a $\sigma$-algebra as a body of information: $\mathcal G\subset\mathcal F$ is the set of
questions about $\omega$ that can be answered with the information in $\mathcal G$. Closure
under complements and countable unions says: if you can decide $A$ you can decide "not $A$";
if you can decide each $A_n$ you can decide "some $A_n$". A filtration
$\mathcal F_0\subset\mathcal F_1\subset\dots$ is information accumulating in time, and
$\mathcal F_t = \sigma(W_s : s\le t)$ is "everything the Brownian path has revealed by time $t$".
</Intuition>

<Definition name="Measurable function, random variable, adapted process">
For $\sigma$-algebras $\mathcal F$ on $\Omega$ and $\mathcal E$ on $E$, a map
$X:\Omega\to E$ is $\mathcal F/\mathcal E$-measurable if $X^{-1}(B) := \{X\in B\}\in\mathcal F$
for every $B\in\mathcal E$. A random variable is an $\mathcal F/\mathcal B(\mathbb{R})$-measurable
map; a random vector, the same with $\mathbb{R}^d$. The $\sigma$-algebra generated by $X$ is
$\sigma(X) = \{X^{-1}(B) : B\in\mathcal B\}$, the smallest one making $X$ measurable. A process
$(X_t)$ is adapted to a filtration $(\mathcal F_t)$ if each $X_t$ is $\mathcal F_t$-measurable.
The distribution (law) of $X$ is the measure $\mathbb{P}_X(B) = \mathbb{P}(X\in B)$ on
$\mathcal B(\mathbb{R}^d)$.
</Definition>

Sums, products, limits, sups and infs of countably many measurable functions are measurable,
and compositions with continuous functions are measurable. In practice everything you write
down is measurable; the only place the notion does work is through $\sigma(X)$ and the next
fact.

<Proposition name="Doob–Dynkin lemma" label="Prop 1">
A random variable $Y$ is $\sigma(X)$-measurable if and only if $Y = g(X)$ for some Borel
measurable $g:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Sketch. If $Y = g(X)$ then $\{Y\in B\} = \{X\in g^{-1}(B)\}\in\sigma(X)$. Conversely, if
$Y = 1_A$ with $A\in\sigma(X)$ then $A = \{X\in B\}$ for some Borel $B$ and $Y = 1_B(X)$.
Linear combinations give the claim for simple $Y$ (finitely many values), and every
$\sigma(X)$-measurable $Y$ is a pointwise limit of simple $\sigma(X)$-measurable $Y_n = g_n(X)$
(Definition 3 below). Take $g = \limsup g_n$ where the limit exists and $0$ elsewhere. The
skipped step is the "simple functions approximate" lemma, which is Definition 3. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

This is why "$Y$ is $\sigma(X)$-measurable" and "$Y$ is a function of $X$" are the same
statement, and why $\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]$ on the
[conditional expectation](/concepts/conditional-expectation) page is a function of $X$.

## The idea

<Definition name="The Lebesgue integral, i.e. expectation" label="Def 3">
Build $\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_\Omega X\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}$ in three steps.

1. For a simple random variable $X = \sum_{k=1}^n a_k 1_{A_k}$ with $a_k\ge 0$ and
   $A_k\in\mathcal F$, set $\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_k a_k\,\mathbb{P}(A_k)$.
2. For $X\ge 0$ measurable, set $\mathbb{E}[X] = \sup\{\mathbb{E}[S] : S \text{ simple},\ 0\le S\le X\}\in[0,\infty]$.
   Equivalently, $\mathbb{E}[X] = \lim_n\mathbb{E}[S_n]$ for the staircase
   $S_n = \min(n, 2^{-n}\lfloor 2^n X\rfloor)$, which increases to $X$ pointwise.
3. For general $X$, write $X = X^+ - X^-$ and set $\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}[X^+] - \mathbb{E}[X^-]$
   whenever at least one is finite; $X$ is integrable ($X\in L^1$) if $\mathbb{E}|X|$ is finite.

The same construction with any measure $\mu$ in place of $\mathbb{P}$ defines $\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu$.
The integral is linear and monotone ($X\le Y$ a.s. implies $\mathbb{E}X\le\mathbb{E}Y$), and
$\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_X$ (change of variables to
the law), which when $\mathbb{P}_X$ has a density $p$ becomes $\int f(x)p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$.
</Definition>

<Intuition>
Riemann slices the domain into intervals and asks what the function does on each; Lebesgue
slices the range into levels and asks how much probability sits at each level. That is why the
Lebesgue integral needs only measurability of the level sets, works for random variables on an
abstract $\Omega$ with no coordinates, and (the real payoff) commutes with monotone and
dominated limits. When the Riemann integral exists the two agree.
</Intuition>

<Theorem name="Monotone convergence" label="Thm 4">
If $0\le X_n\uparrow X$ almost surely, then $\mathbb{E}[X_n]\uparrow\mathbb{E}[X]$, with
$\infty$ allowed on both sides.
</Theorem>

The proof (Williams 5.3, about a page) uses continuity of $\mathbb{P}$ along increasing
events and the staircase of Def 3; we take it as the one theorem imported from the
construction. Everything else follows from it.

<Lemma name="Fatou" label="Lemma 5">
If $X_n\ge 0$ then $\mathbb{E}[\liminf_n X_n]\le\liminf_n\mathbb{E}[X_n]$.
</Lemma>

<Proof>
Let $G_n = \inf_{k\ge n}X_k$. Then $0\le G_n\uparrow\liminf_k X_k$ and $G_n\le X_n$. By
Thm 4, $\mathbb{E}[\liminf X_k] = \lim_n\mathbb{E}[G_n]$, and
$\mathbb{E}[G_n]\le\mathbb{E}[X_n]$ for each $n$, so the limit is at most
$\liminf_n\mathbb{E}[X_n]$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Theorem name="Dominated convergence" label="Thm 6">
If $X_n\to X$ almost surely and there is an integrable $Y$ with $|X_n|\le Y$ for all $n$, then
$X$ is integrable, $\mathbb{E}[X_n]\to\mathbb{E}[X]$, and in fact $\mathbb{E}|X_n - X|\to 0$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
$|X|\le Y$ a.s., so $X$ is integrable. Both $Y - X_n$ and $Y + X_n$ are nonnegative, so Fatou
applies to each:

$$
\mathbb{E}[Y - X] \le \liminf_n\mathbb{E}[Y - X_n] = \mathbb{E}[Y] - \limsup_n\mathbb{E}[X_n],
\qquad
\mathbb{E}[Y + X] \le \mathbb{E}[Y] + \liminf_n\mathbb{E}[X_n].
$$

Cancel the finite $\mathbb{E}[Y]$: $\limsup\mathbb{E}[X_n]\le\mathbb{E}[X]\le\liminf\mathbb{E}[X_n]$,
so the limit exists and equals $\mathbb{E}[X]$. For the last claim apply what was just proved
to $|X_n - X|\to 0$, which is dominated by $2Y$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Corollary name="Differentiating under the expectation" label="Cor 7">
Let $f(\theta,\omega)$ be differentiable in $\theta\in(a,b)$ for every $\omega$, with
$|\partial_\theta f(\theta,\omega)|\le G(\omega)$ for all $\theta$ and $\mathbb{E}[G]$ finite.
Then $F(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[f(\theta,\cdot)]$ is differentiable and
$F'(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[\partial_\theta f(\theta,\cdot)]$.
</Corollary>

<Proof>
For $h\to 0$, the difference quotient $(f(\theta+h,\omega) - f(\theta,\omega))/h$ equals
$\partial_\theta f(\xi,\omega)$ for some $\xi$ between $\theta$ and $\theta+h$ (mean value
theorem), hence is bounded by $G(\omega)$, and it converges pointwise to
$\partial_\theta f(\theta,\omega)$. Apply Thm 6 along any sequence $h_n\to 0$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Definition name="Modes of convergence" label="Def 8">
$X_n\to X$ almost surely if $\mathbb{P}(\lim_n X_n = X) = 1$; in probability if
$\mathbb{P}(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon)\to 0$ for every $\varepsilon > 0$; in $L^p$ ($p\ge 1$) if
$\mathbb{E}|X_n - X|^p\to 0$.
</Definition>

<Proposition name="Relations between the modes" label="Prop 9">
1. (Markov's inequality) For $X\ge 0$ and $a > 0$, $\mathbb{P}(X\ge a)\le\mathbb{E}[X]/a$.
2. $L^p$ convergence implies convergence in probability.
3. Almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability.
4. Convergence in probability implies almost sure convergence along a subsequence.
5. Neither a.s. nor $L^p$ convergence implies the other.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
(1) $a\,1_{\{X\ge a\}}\le X$ pointwise; take expectations.
(2) $\mathbb{P}(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon) = \mathbb{P}(|X_n - X|^p > \varepsilon^p)\le\mathbb{E}|X_n-X|^p/\varepsilon^p\to 0$.
(3) Let $A_n = \{\sup_{k\ge n}|X_k - X| > \varepsilon\}$. The $A_n$ decrease and their
intersection is contained in the null event $\{X_n\not\to X\}$, so $\mathbb{P}(A_n)\to 0$ by
countable additivity (continuity from above), and $\mathbb{P}(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon)\le\mathbb{P}(A_n)$.
(4) Pick $n_k$ with $\mathbb{P}(|X_{n_k} - X| > 2^{-k})\le 2^{-k}$. The events
$B_k = \{|X_{n_k} - X| > 2^{-k}\}$ have $\sum_k\mathbb{P}(B_k)$ finite, so by Borel–Cantelli
($\mathbb{P}(\bigcup_{k\ge m}B_k)\le\sum_{k\ge m}\mathbb{P}(B_k)\to 0$) almost every $\omega$
lies in only finitely many $B_k$, i.e. $|X_{n_k}-X|\le 2^{-k}$ eventually, so $X_{n_k}\to X$ a.s.
(5) Two examples, both on $U\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]$. $X_n = n\,1_{\{U\le 1/n\}}$ converges to
$0$ a.s. but $\mathbb{E}|X_n| = 1$ (worked below). The typewriter sequence
$X_n = 1_{[j2^{-m},(j+1)2^{-m}]}(U)$ for $n = 2^m + j$, $0\le j$ smaller than $2^m$, has
$\mathbb{E}|X_n| = 2^{-m}\to 0$ but for every $\omega$ the sequence $X_n(\omega)$ equals $1$
infinitely often, so it does not converge a.s. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Definition name="Absolute continuity" label="Def 10">
For measures $\mu,\nu$ on the same $(\Omega,\mathcal F)$, $\nu$ is absolutely continuous with
respect to $\mu$, written $\nu\ll\mu$, if $\mu(A) = 0$ implies $\nu(A) = 0$. They are
equivalent, $\nu\sim\mu$, if also $\mu\ll\nu$. A measure is $\sigma$-finite if $\Omega$ is a
countable union of sets of finite measure (Lebesgue measure and every probability are).
</Definition>

<Theorem name="Radon–Nikodym" label="Thm 11">
Let $\mu,\nu$ be $\sigma$-finite with $\nu\ll\mu$. Then there is a measurable
$f:\Omega\to[0,\infty)$, unique up to $\mu$-null sets, with
$\nu(A) = \int_A f\,\mathrm{d}\mu$ for all $A\in\mathcal F$. It is written
$f = \mathrm{d}\nu/\mathrm{d}\mu$, and for every $\nu$-integrable $g$,
$$
\int g\,\mathrm{d}\nu = \int g\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\mu .
$$
If $\rho\ll\nu\ll\mu$ then $\mathrm{d}\rho/\mathrm{d}\mu = (\mathrm{d}\rho/\mathrm{d}\nu)(\mathrm{d}\nu/\mathrm{d}\mu)$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Sketch. Existence is the hard part; von Neumann's proof considers the linear functional
$g\mapsto\int g\,\mathrm{d}\nu$ on the Hilbert space $L^2(\mu+\nu)$, represents it as an
inner product with some $h$ by the Riesz representation theorem, and solves for $f = h/(1-h)$
using $\nu\ll\mu$ to show $h$ is less than $1$ a.e. Uniqueness: if $f,f'$ both work,
integrate over $A = \{f > f'\}$ to get $\int_A(f-f')\,\mathrm{d}\mu = 0$ with a strictly
positive integrand, forcing $\mu(A) = 0$. The integral formula holds for $g = 1_A$ by
definition, for simple $g$ by linearity, for $g\ge 0$ by Thm 4 applied to the staircase, and
for general $g$ by splitting into $g^\pm$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

Three readings of the theorem, all used constantly.

- A density is a Radon–Nikodym derivative: $X$ "has density $p$" means
  $\mathbb{P}_X\ll\lambda$ and $p = \mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_X/\mathrm{d}\lambda$, i.e.
  $\mathbb{P}(X\in A) = \int_A p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$. A discrete $X$ has $\mathbb{P}_X\not\ll\lambda$
  (a point has Lebesgue measure zero but positive probability) and therefore no density; a
  Gaussian with singular covariance in $\mathbb{R}^d$ likewise. -
A likelihood ratio is a Radon–Nikodym derivative: if $\mathbb{Q}\ll\mathbb{P}$ on $\Omega$
  then $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[g] = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[g\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}/\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}]$
  (importance sampling), and when both have densities $q,p$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$ the chain rule
  gives $\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}/\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P} = q/p$. -
KL divergence is defined by it: $\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}) =
  \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\log\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}/\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}]$ if $\mathbb{Q}\ll\mathbb{P}$
  and $+\infty$ otherwise. This is the only definition that makes sense for measures on path
  space, where there is no Lebesgue measure to take densities against; see
  [Girsanov](/concepts/girsanov-theorem).

## Worked example by hand

<Example title="Two coin flips">
$\Omega = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$, $\mathcal F$ all $16$ subsets, $\mathbb{P}$ uniform. Let $X$
be the number of heads and $F$ the indicator that the first flip is heads.

Information. $\sigma(X)$ is generated by the level sets $\{X = 0\} = \{TT\}$,
$\{X = 1\} = \{HT,TH\}$, $\{X = 2\} = \{HH\}$: it consists of the $2^3 = 8$ unions of these
three blocks. $\sigma(F) = \{\emptyset, \{HH,HT\}, \{TH,TT\}, \Omega\}$. Is $F$ a function of
$X$? By Prop 1 it would have to be $\sigma(X)$-measurable, but $\{F = 1\} = \{HH,HT\}$ is not a
union of the three blocks. No. Is $X$ a function of $F$? $\{X = 2\} = \{HH\}\notin\sigma(F)$. No.
Knowing the count does not tell you the first flip, and conversely.

Expectation as a Lebesgue integral. $X = 0\cdot 1_{\{TT\}} + 1\cdot 1_{\{HT,TH\}} + 2\cdot 1_{\{HH\}}$
is simple, so by Def 3 step 1, $\mathbb{E}[X] = 0\cdot\tfrac14 + 1\cdot\tfrac12 + 2\cdot\tfrac14 = 1$.
Check: $X$ is Binomial$(2,\tfrac12)$ with mean $2\cdot\tfrac12 = 1$.

Radon–Nikodym. Let $\mathbb{Q}$ be the law of two independent flips with heads probability
$0.6$: $\mathbb{Q}(HH) = 0.36$, $\mathbb{Q}(HT) = \mathbb{Q}(TH) = 0.24$, $\mathbb{Q}(TT) = 0.16$.
Every set of $\mathbb{P}$-measure zero is empty, so $\mathbb{Q}\ll\mathbb{P}$, and on a finite
space the derivative is the ratio of point masses:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}(HH) = \frac{0.36}{0.25} = 1.44,\quad
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}(HT) = \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}(TH) = 0.96,\quad
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}(TT) = 0.64 .
$$

Check that it is a density: $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}/\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}]
= \tfrac14(1.44 + 0.96 + 0.96 + 0.64) = 1$. Use it: $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[X]
= \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[X\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}/\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}]
= \tfrac14(2\cdot 1.44 + 0.96 + 0.96 + 0) = 1.2$, which is $2\cdot 0.6$. Finally

$$
\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}) = 0.36\log 1.44 + 2\cdot 0.24\log 0.96 + 0.16\log 0.64
= 0.1313 - 0.0196 - 0.0714 = 0.0403 .
$$

Check against the KL of a single biased flip, $0.6\log 1.2 + 0.4\log 0.8 = 0.0201$: two
independent flips give twice that, $0.0403$. Good. This additivity is the chain rule of KL,
proved on [KL divergence](/concepts/kl-divergence-and-variational-bounds).
</Example>

<Example title="When you may not swap limit and expectation">
Let $U\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]$ and $X_n = n\,1_{\{U\le 1/n\}}$. For any outcome with $U > 0$,
$X_n(\omega) = 0$ as soon as $n > 1/U$, so $X_n\to 0$ almost surely (and in probability, since
$\mathbb{P}(X_n\ne 0) = 1/n$). But $\mathbb{E}[X_n] = n\cdot\tfrac1n = 1$ for every $n$:

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[\lim_n X_n\big] = 0 \ne 1 = \lim_n\mathbb{E}[X_n].
$$

Fatou is respected ($0\le 1$), and Thm 6 is not violated because no integrable dominator
exists: on the event $U\in(\tfrac1{k+1},\tfrac1k]$ we have $X_k = k$, so
$\sup_n X_n\ge k\ge\tfrac{1}{2U}$ whenever $U\le\tfrac12$, and
$\mathbb{E}[\tfrac{1}{2U}1_{\{U\le 1/2\}}] = \tfrac12\int_0^{1/2}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}u}{u} = \infty$.
A tall thin spike carries unit mass to zero width. The same phenomenon is unbounded importance
weights in off-policy RL: $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[g\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}/\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}]$
is exact, but a Monte Carlo estimate of it can have infinite variance.
</Example>

## Where it shows up

- The [Itô integral](/concepts/stochastic-integral) is defined for simple adapted integrands
  and extended to $L^2$ integrands as an $L^2$ limit; "adapted" is measurability with respect
  to $\mathcal F_t$, and the extension is Prop 9 style bookkeeping with the Itô isometry.
- [Girsanov's theorem](/concepts/girsanov-theorem) is a Radon–Nikodym derivative between the
  laws of two SDEs on path space. De Bortoli et al., <Cite id="2106.01357" />, define the
  Schrödinger bridge as $\arg\min\{\mathrm{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\mathbb{P}) : \pi\in\mathcal P\}$ over
  path measures with fixed marginals; without Thm 11 that objective is not even defined. -
Song et al., <Cite id="2011.13456" />, write $p_t$ for the marginal density of $X_t$ and
  $\nabla\log p_t$ for its score. That $p_t$ exists is $\mathbb{P}_{X_t}\ll\lambda$, guaranteed
  when the diffusion coefficient is nondegenerate.
- The [policy gradient theorem](/concepts/policy-gradient-theorem) starts with
  $\nabla_\theta\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}[R(\tau)] = \mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(\tau)R(\tau)]$,
  which is Cor 7 applied to the likelihood ratio $\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_\theta/\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{\theta_0}$. -
The ELBO and every "$\mathbb{E}_q[\log p/q]$" on the
  [KL divergence](/concepts/kl-divergence-and-variational-bounds) page is
  $\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log\mathrm{d}q/\mathrm{d}p]$.

## Exercises

<Exercise hint="Write the events {X in B} for B Borel and notice that only whether B contains 0 and whether it contains 1 matters.">
Let $X = 1_A$ for an event $A$. Show $\sigma(X) = \{\emptyset, A, A^c, \Omega\}$, and describe
$\sigma(X,Y)$ for $Y = 1_B$ with $B$ another event (at most $16$ sets).
</Exercise>

<Exercise hint="Chebyshev is Markov applied to (X minus its mean) squared; for the second part use Prop 9(2) with p = 2.">
Prove Chebyshev's inequality $\mathbb{P}(|X - \mathbb{E}X|\ge a)\le\operatorname{Var}(X)/a^2$
from Prop 9(1), and use it to show the weak law of large numbers for i.i.d. $X_i$ with finite
variance: the sample mean converges in probability.
</Exercise>

<Exercise hint="Define the integral for indicators first; every step of Def 3 is a monotone limit, and Thm 4 handles the limits.">
Let $\mathbb{Q}(A) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[Z 1_A]$ for a random variable $Z\ge 0$ with
$\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[Z] = 1$. Show $\mathbb{Q}$ is a probability measure with
$\mathbb{Q}\ll\mathbb{P}$ and $\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}/\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P} = Z$, and prove
$\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[g] = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[gZ]$ for every bounded $g$. This is
the form in which Girsanov's theorem is stated.
</Exercise>

<Exercise hint="The integrand has size n on a set of measure 1/n; compare with the spike example and compute E of the sup.">
Let $X_n = \sqrt n\,1_{\{U\le 1/n\}}$. Determine in which of the three modes $X_n\to 0$, and
whether $\mathbb{E}[X_n]\to\mathbb{E}[0]$. Then find an integrable dominator or prove none
exists.
</Exercise>

## Going deeper

Williams, *Probability with Martingales*, Ch. 1-6, is the fastest honest route: it builds
exactly what is above with full proofs in under 60 pages, and its Ch. 5.14 is the proof of
Radon–Nikodym via martingales. Durrett Ch. 1 and Appendix A cover the same ground with the
construction of Lebesgue measure. Le Gall's book is the reference when you also want the
stochastic process side (Ch. 8 onward) in the same notation.
