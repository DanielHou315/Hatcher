## Why you need this

Every denoiser is a conditional expectation. The DDPM network $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)$
trained with $\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\|^2$ (Ho et al. 2020,
eq. 14) is an estimate of $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid x_t]$; Vincent's 2011 result that
denoising score matching has the same minimizer as explicit score matching, and Theorem 2 of
Lipman et al. 2022 that the conditional flow matching loss has the same gradient as the
marginal one, are both the statement that regressing on a noisy target recovers the conditional
expectation of that target. The Bellman equation
$Q^\pi(s,a) = r(s,a) + \gamma\,\mathbb{E}[V^\pi(S')\mid S = s, A = a]$ is a conditional
expectation, and the martingale property, defined through conditional expectation, is what
makes the Itô integral and Girsanov's density process work. This page gives the definition
that covers all of these at once and the one computation (Gaussian conditioning) you will
repeat most.

## Setup

Fix $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb{P})$ and a sub-$\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal G\subset\mathcal F$,
read as partial information (see
[measure-theoretic probability](/concepts/measure-theoretic-probability)). Let $L^2$ be the
space of random variables with $\mathbb{E}X^2$ finite, with inner product
$\langle X,Y\rangle = \mathbb{E}[XY]$ and norm $\|X\|_2 = \sqrt{\mathbb{E}X^2}$; two random
variables that agree almost surely are identified. Let $L^2(\mathcal G)\subset L^2$ be the
$\mathcal G$-measurable ones.

Two Hilbert-space facts are used. $L^2$ is complete (Riesz–Fischer), and $L^2(\mathcal G)$ is a
closed subspace: an $L^2$ limit of $\mathcal G$-measurable variables has an a.s.-convergent
subsequence, and a.s. limits of $\mathcal G$-measurable variables are $\mathcal G$-measurable.
Hence the projection theorem applies: for every $X\in L^2$ there is a unique $Y\in L^2(\mathcal G)$
minimizing $\|X - Y\|_2$, characterized by $\langle X - Y, Z\rangle = 0$ for all
$Z\in L^2(\mathcal G)$. (Proof in two lines: a minimizing sequence is Cauchy by the
parallelogram law; completeness gives the limit; if the residual were not orthogonal to some
$Z$, moving a little along $Z$ would reduce the distance.)

## The idea

<Definition name="Conditional expectation">
For integrable $X$, a conditional expectation of $X$ given $\mathcal G$ is any integrable
random variable $Y$ such that

1. $Y$ is $\mathcal G$-measurable, and
2. $\mathbb{E}[Y 1_A] = \mathbb{E}[X 1_A]$ for every $A\in\mathcal G$.

It is written $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal G]$. Condition 1 says $Y$ uses only the information in
$\mathcal G$; condition 2 says $Y$ has the same averages as $X$ on every event $\mathcal G$ can
see.
</Definition>

<Theorem name="Existence, uniqueness, and the projection characterization" label="Thm 1">
For every integrable $X$ a conditional expectation exists and is unique almost surely. For
$X\in L^2$ it is the orthogonal projection of $X$ onto $L^2(\mathcal G)$, and it is the unique
minimizer of $\mathbb{E}(X - Y)^2$ over $Y\in L^2(\mathcal G)$; moreover, for every such $Y$,

$$
\mathbb{E}(X - Y)^2 = \mathbb{E}\big(X - \mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal G]\big)^2 + \mathbb{E}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal G] - Y\big)^2 .
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
$L^2$ case. Let $Y$ be the projection of $X$ onto $L^2(\mathcal G)$. It is
$\mathcal G$-measurable and integrable, and orthogonality with $Z = 1_A$ for $A\in\mathcal G$
reads $\mathbb{E}[(X - Y)1_A] = 0$, which is condition 2. For the identity, take any
$Z\in L^2(\mathcal G)$ and expand $X - Z = (X - Y) + (Y - Z)$: the cross term
$\mathbb{E}[(X-Y)(Y-Z)]$ vanishes because $Y - Z\in L^2(\mathcal G)$. This is Pythagoras, and it
shows $Y$ is the unique minimizer.

Uniqueness in general. If $Y,Y'$ both satisfy the definition, then $A = \{Y > Y'\}\in\mathcal G$
and $\mathbb{E}[(Y - Y')1_A] = \mathbb{E}[X1_A] - \mathbb{E}[X1_A] = 0$ with a nonnegative
integrand that is positive on $A$, so $\mathbb{P}(A) = 0$; symmetrically for $\{Y' > Y\}$.

Extension to $L^1$. First positivity: if $X\ge 0$ then $Y = \mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal G]\ge 0$
a.s., because $A = \{Y \lt 0\}\in\mathcal G$ gives $\mathbb{E}[Y1_A] = \mathbb{E}[X1_A]\ge 0$
while the left side is at most $0$, forcing $\mathbb{P}(A) = 0$. By linearity of the defining
condition, $X\le X'$ implies $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal G]\le\mathbb{E}[X'\mid\mathcal G]$. Now
for integrable $X\ge 0$ let $X_n = \min(X,n)\in L^2$ and $Y_n = \mathbb{E}[X_n\mid\mathcal G]$,
an increasing sequence; set $Y = \lim Y_n$, which is $\mathcal G$-measurable. Monotone
convergence on both sides of $\mathbb{E}[Y_n1_A] = \mathbb{E}[X_n1_A]$ gives
$\mathbb{E}[Y1_A] = \mathbb{E}[X1_A]$, and with $A = \Omega$ that $Y$ is integrable. For
general $X$ apply this to $X^+$ and $X^-$ and subtract. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Intuition>
$\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal G]$ is the best guess of $X$ using only the information in
$\mathcal G$, where "best" means least squares. The Pythagorean identity says the error of any
other $\mathcal G$-measurable guess splits into an irreducible part (how much $X$ varies beyond
what $\mathcal G$ knows) and the distance of the guess from the best one. Training a network on
noisy targets therefore does not chase noise: the irreducible part is a constant.
</Intuition>

<Proposition name="Rules of calculation" label="Prop 2">
Let $X, X'$ be integrable and $\mathcal G\subset\mathcal H\subset\mathcal F$.

1. Linearity: $\mathbb{E}[aX + bX'\mid\mathcal G] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal G] + b\,\mathbb{E}[X'\mid\mathcal G]$.
2. Total expectation: $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal G]\big] = \mathbb{E}[X]$.
3. Tower: $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal H]\,\big|\,\mathcal G\big] = \mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal G]$
   (the coarser information wins).
4. Taking out what is known: if $Z$ is $\mathcal G$-measurable and $ZX$ is integrable,
   $\mathbb{E}[ZX\mid\mathcal G] = Z\,\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal G]$.
5. Independence: if $X$ is independent of $\mathcal G$, $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal G] = \mathbb{E}[X]$.
6. Conditional Jensen: for convex $\varphi$ with $\varphi(X)$ integrable,
   $\varphi(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal G])\le\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)\mid\mathcal G]$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
(1) The defining conditions are linear in $X$. (2) Condition 2 with $A = \Omega$.
(3) $Y = \mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal G]$ is $\mathcal G$-measurable. For $A\in\mathcal G\subset\mathcal H$,
$\mathbb{E}[Y1_A] = \mathbb{E}[X1_A] = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal H]1_A\big]$,
the last equality because $A\in\mathcal H$. So $Y$ satisfies the definition of
$\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal H]\mid\mathcal G]$, and uniqueness finishes.
(4) For $Z = 1_B$ with $B\in\mathcal G$ and any $A\in\mathcal G$:
$\mathbb{E}[1_B\,\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal G]\,1_A] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal G]1_{A\cap B}]
= \mathbb{E}[X1_{A\cap B}] = \mathbb{E}[(1_BX)1_A]$ since $A\cap B\in\mathcal G$. Linearity
extends this to simple $Z$, monotone convergence to $Z\ge 0$, and splitting to general $Z$.
(5) $\mathbb{E}[X1_A] = \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{P}(A) = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X]\,1_A]$ for
$A\in\mathcal G$, and the constant $\mathbb{E}[X]$ is $\mathcal G$-measurable.
(6) Write $\varphi$ as the supremum of the countably many affine functions below it and use
(1) and positivity; Williams 9.7(h) has the details. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Proposition name="Conditioning on a random variable" label="Prop 3">
For a random vector $Z$, define $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Z] := \mathbb{E}[X\mid\sigma(Z)]$. By the
Doob–Dynkin lemma there is a measurable $g$ with $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Z] = g(Z)$, and one writes
$\mathbb{E}[X\mid Z = z] := g(z)$. If $(X,Z)$ has a joint density $p(x,z)$ with marginal
$p_Z(z) > 0$, then

$$
g(z) = \int x\,p(x\mid z)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad p(x\mid z) = \frac{p(x,z)}{p_Z(z)} .
$$
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Events in $\sigma(Z)$ are of the form $\{Z\in B\}$. With $g$ as displayed,

$$
\mathbb{E}[g(Z)1_B(Z)] = \int_B g(z)p_Z(z)\,\mathrm{d}z = \int_B\!\int x\,p(x,z)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}z
= \mathbb{E}[X\,1_B(Z)],
$$

so $g(Z)$ satisfies the definition; it is $\sigma(Z)$-measurable by Doob–Dynkin. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Theorem name="Regression on a noisy target" label="Thm 4">
Let $X\in L^2$ (a random vector, componentwise) and $Z$ a random vector, and let
$g^\star(z) = \mathbb{E}[X\mid Z = z]$. Then for every measurable $g$ with $g(Z)\in L^2$,

$$
\mathbb{E}\|X - g(Z)\|^2 = \mathbb{E}\|X - g^\star(Z)\|^2 + \mathbb{E}\|g^\star(Z) - g(Z)\|^2 .
$$

In particular, minimizing $\mathbb{E}\|X - g_\theta(Z)\|^2$ over a parametric family and
minimizing $\mathbb{E}\|g^\star(Z) - g_\theta(Z)\|^2$ give the same loss up to an additive
constant, the same gradients in $\theta$, and the same minimizers.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Apply the identity of Thm 1 to each component of $X$ with $\mathcal G = \sigma(Z)$, noting
that $g(Z)$ is $\sigma(Z)$-measurable, and add. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Definition name="Martingale">
Let $(\mathcal F_t)$ be a filtration (indexed by $t\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}$ or $t\ge 0$). An
adapted, integrable process $M$ is a martingale if
$\mathbb{E}[M_t\mid\mathcal F_s] = M_s$ for all $s\le t$; a submartingale if $\ge$, a
supermartingale if $\le$. Equivalently, $\mathbb{E}[M_t - M_s\mid\mathcal F_s] = 0$: given
the past, the future increment has mean zero.
</Definition>

<Proposition name="Three facts about martingales" label="Prop 5">
1. $\mathbb{E}[M_t] = \mathbb{E}[M_0]$ for all $t$.
2. (Doob martingale) For integrable $X$, $M_t = \mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal F_t]$ is a martingale.
3. (Orthogonal increments) If $M$ is a square-integrable martingale and $r\le s\le t$, then
   $\mathbb{E}[(M_t - M_s)(M_s - M_r)] = 0$, so
   $\mathbb{E}[(M_t - M_0)^2] = \sum_k\mathbb{E}[(M_{t_{k+1}} - M_{t_k})^2]$ over any partition.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
(1) Prop 2(2). (2) Tower: $\mathbb{E}[M_t\mid\mathcal F_s] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal F_t]\mid\mathcal F_s]
= \mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal F_s] = M_s$ since $\mathcal F_s\subset\mathcal F_t$.
(3) $\mathbb{E}[(M_t - M_s)(M_s - M_r)] = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[M_t - M_s\mid\mathcal F_s](M_s - M_r)\big] = 0$,
using Prop 2(2), then (4) to pull the $\mathcal F_s$-measurable factor $M_s - M_r$ inside, then
the martingale property. Expanding the square of the telescoping sum, all cross terms vanish.
$\blacksquare$
</Proof>

Fact 3 is the reason the Itô isometry holds: the
[Itô integral](/concepts/stochastic-integral) of a simple process is a martingale transform,
and its second moment is a sum of second moments of increments with no cross terms.

## Worked example by hand

<Example title="Gaussian conditioning as a projection">
Let $(X,Z)$ be jointly Gaussian with $\mathbb{E}X = 1$, $\mathbb{E}Z = 2$,
$\operatorname{Var}X = 4$, $\operatorname{Var}Z = 3$, $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Z) = 2$. We
compute $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Z]$ directly from Thm 1, without the conditional density.

Step 1: project onto the span of $\{1, Z\}$. Look for $Y = a + bZ$ with residual
$R = X - a - bZ$ orthogonal to $1$ and to $Z$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[R] = 1 - a - 2b = 0,\qquad
\mathbb{E}[RZ] = \mathbb{E}[XZ] - a\,\mathbb{E}Z - b\,\mathbb{E}Z^2 = 4 - 2a - 7b = 0,
$$

using $\mathbb{E}[XZ] = \operatorname{Cov}(X,Z) + \mathbb{E}X\,\mathbb{E}Z = 2 + 2 = 4$ and
$\mathbb{E}Z^2 = 3 + 4 = 7$. Substituting $a = 1 - 2b$ into the second equation:
$4 - 2 + 4b - 7b = 0$, so $b = 2/3$ and $a = -1/3$. Thus $Y = -\tfrac13 + \tfrac23 Z = 1 + \tfrac23(Z - 2)$.

Step 2: show $Y$ is the projection onto all of $L^2(\sigma(Z))$, not just the span of $\{1,Z\}$.
The pair $(R,Z)$ is an affine image of $(X,Z)$, hence jointly Gaussian, and
$\operatorname{Cov}(R,Z) = \mathbb{E}[RZ] - \mathbb{E}R\,\mathbb{E}Z = 0$ by construction. For
Gaussians, uncorrelated means independent
([Gaussian computations](/concepts/gaussian-computations), Prop 2). So for every measurable
$h$ with $h(Z)\in L^2$, $\mathbb{E}[R\,h(Z)] = \mathbb{E}[R]\,\mathbb{E}[h(Z)] = 0$. The residual is
orthogonal to everything $\sigma(Z)$-measurable, so by Thm 1

$$
\mathbb{E}[X\mid Z] = 1 + \tfrac23(Z - 2),\qquad
\mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}[X\mid Z])^2\big] = \mathbb{E}R^2 = 4 - b^2\cdot 3 = 4 - \tfrac43 = \tfrac83 .
$$

Checks. This agrees with the Schur complement formula
$\mu_X + \Sigma_{XZ}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{-1}(z - \mu_Z)$ and $\Sigma_{XX} - \Sigma_{XZ}\Sigma_{ZZ}^{-1}\Sigma_{ZX} = 4 - 4/3$.
Pythagoras with $Y = \mathbb{E}X$ constant:
$\operatorname{Var}X = 4 = \mathbb{E}R^2 + \operatorname{Var}(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Z]) = \tfrac83 + \tfrac49\cdot 3 = \tfrac83 + \tfrac43$. Good.
For a non-Gaussian pair Step 1 still gives the best linear predictor, but Step 2 fails and
$\mathbb{E}[X\mid Z]$ is in general nonlinear; this is why Kalman filtering is exact only for
Gaussian noise.
</Example>

<Example title="The optimal denoiser and its irreducible loss">
Data $X\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, noise $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ independent, observation
$Z = X + \sigma\varepsilon$ with $\sigma = 1$. Then $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Z) = 1$,
$\operatorname{Var}Z = 2$, and Step 1 above gives $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Z] = Z/2$ (the means are
zero, so $a = 0$). The optimal noise predictor is
$\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid Z] = (Z - \mathbb{E}[X\mid Z])/\sigma = Z/2$ too.

Tweedie's formula says $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Z = z] = z + \sigma^2\nabla_z\log p_Z(z)$. Here
$p_Z = \mathcal N(0,2)$, so $\nabla\log p_Z(z) = -z/2$ and $z + 1\cdot(-z/2) = z/2$. Agreed.

The minimal loss is $\mathbb{E}(X - Z/2)^2 = \operatorname{Var}X - \operatorname{Cov}(X,Z)^2/\operatorname{Var}Z = 1 - \tfrac12 = \tfrac12$.
A perfectly trained DDPM at this noise level reports a mean squared error of $\tfrac12$ on
$\varepsilon$, not $0$: by Thm 4 the loss of the best network is the irreducible term
$\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon - \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid x_t]\|^2$, and the network's distance from
$\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid x_t]$ is what training actually drives to zero.
</Example>

<Example title="Two coin flips: tower and martingale">
$\Omega = \{HH,HT,TH,TT\}$ uniform, $X$ = number of heads, $\mathcal F_1 = \sigma(\text{first flip})$,
$\mathcal F_0$ trivial, $\mathcal F_2$ everything. Guess $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal F_1] = 1_{\{\text{first} = H\}} + \tfrac12$.
Check condition 2 on $A = \{HH,HT\}$: $\mathbb{E}[X1_A] = (2 + 1)/4 = 3/4$ and
$\mathbb{E}[(1 + \tfrac12)1_A] = \tfrac32\cdot\tfrac12 = 3/4$; on $A^c$ both sides are $1/4$.
Tower: $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal F_1]] = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1 = \mathbb{E}X$.
The Doob martingale is $M_0 = 1$, $M_1 = \tfrac12 + 1_{\{\text{first} = H\}}$, $M_2 = X$: the
running best estimate of the final count, whose increments $\pm\tfrac12$ have mean zero given
the past.
</Example>

## Where it shows up

- DDPM, <Cite id="2006.11239" />: the simplified loss (eq. 14) is Thm 4 with $X = \varepsilon$
  and $Z = (x_t,t)$. Vincent 2011 proves denoising score matching equals explicit score
  matching up to a constant; that is the Pythagorean identity of Thm 4 with $X = \nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t\mid x_0)$,
  whose conditional expectation given $x_t$ is $\nabla\log q_t(x_t)$. See
  [score matching](/concepts/score-matching).
- Flow matching, <Cite id="2210.02747" />: the marginal vector field
  $u_t(x) = \int u_t(x\mid x_1)\frac{p_t(x\mid x_1)q(x_1)}{p_t(x)}\mathrm{d}x_1$ (their eq. 8)
  is Prop 3: $u_t(x) = \mathbb{E}[u_t(X_t\mid X_1)\mid X_t = x]$. Their Theorem 2 (the CFM and
  FM losses have equal gradients) is Thm 4 with $Z = X_t$. Worked in full on
  [flow matching](/concepts/flow-matching).
- Bellman operators on [MDPs](/concepts/mdp-and-bellman-operators):
  $(T^\pi V)(s) = \mathbb{E}[r + \gamma V(S')\mid S = s]$ is a conditional expectation, and TD
  learning is a stochastic approximation of it; the TD error has conditional mean zero at the
  fixed point, which is a martingale-difference statement.
- The Itô integral is a martingale, and Girsanov's density
  $\exp(\int\theta\,\mathrm{d}W - \tfrac12\int\theta^2\mathrm{d}t)$ is a martingale; both use
  Prop 5. See [Girsanov](/concepts/girsanov-theorem). -
The Kalman filter state estimate is $\mathbb{E}[x_t\mid y_{1:t}]$, computed recursively by
  the Gaussian example above.

## Exercises

<Exercise hint="Apply Prop 2(2) to the square of X minus E[X | G], expand, and use Prop 2(4) on the cross term.">
Prove the law of total variance: $\operatorname{Var}X = \mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid\mathcal G)] + \operatorname{Var}(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal G])$,
where $\operatorname{Var}(X\mid\mathcal G) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal G])^2\mid\mathcal G]$.
</Exercise>

<Exercise hint="Write W_t as W_s plus an independent increment; use Prop 2(4) for the W_s terms and Prop 2(5) for the increment.">
Let $W$ be Brownian motion with its natural filtration. Show directly from the definition and
Prop 2 that $W_t$ and $W_t^2 - t$ are martingales.
</Exercise>

<Exercise hint="Thm 4 with Z = X_t and X = u_t(X_t | X_1); the constant is the irreducible term.">
In flow matching, $X_1\sim q$, $X_t\mid X_1\sim p_t(\cdot\mid X_1)$, and
$u_t(x\mid x_1)$ is a given conditional vector field. Show that for any $v$,
$\mathbb{E}\|v(X_t) - u_t(X_t\mid X_1)\|^2 = \mathbb{E}\|v(X_t) - u_t(X_t)\|^2 + C$ with
$u_t(x) = \mathbb{E}[u_t(X_t\mid X_1)\mid X_t = x]$ and $C$ independent of $v$.
</Exercise>

<Exercise hint="Compute E[X | Z] from the joint density by Prop 3; the answer is not affine in z.">
Let $X$ be uniform on $\{-1, +1\}$ and $Z = X + \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$
independent. Show $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Z = z] = \tanh(z)$, and compare with the best linear
predictor $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Z)Z/\operatorname{Var}Z = z/2$ at $z = 3$.
</Exercise>

## Going deeper

Williams Ch. 9 is the source of the proof above (the $L^2$ projection followed by the
monotone extension) and lists every rule of Prop 2 with proofs; Ch. 10-12 develop martingales,
optional stopping, and the convergence theorems that stochastic approximation proofs rely on.
Durrett Ch. 4.1 gives the alternative existence proof via Radon–Nikodym, which is worth seeing
once: $A\mapsto\mathbb{E}[X1_A]$ is a measure on $\mathcal G$ absolutely continuous with
respect to $\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal G}$, and $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal G]$ is its density.
