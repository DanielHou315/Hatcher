## Why you need this

A diffusion model's forward SDE turns data into noise. Sampling means running it backwards, and
Anderson's 1982 theorem says exactly what "backwards" is: another SDE, with the same noise level,
whose drift is the forward drift reversed plus $g^2\nabla\log p_t$. That is Eq. (6) of
<Cite id="2011.13456" />, and the reason the score is the only unknown a diffusion model has to
learn. The same computation, with the noise switched off, gives the probability flow ODE
(their Eq. 13), which DDIM, the samplers of <Cite id="2206.00364" />, and the flow-matching
samplers of $\pi_0$ integrate. Diffusion Policy (<Cite id="2303.04137" />) samples robot actions by
discretising this reverse process conditioned on observations.

## Setup

On $[0,T]$ let $X$ solve the forward SDE

$$
\mathrm{d}X_t = f(X_t,t)\,\mathrm{d}t + g(t)\,\mathrm{d}W_t , \qquad X_0\sim p_0 ,
$$

with $f$ locally Lipschitz of linear growth, $g(t)>0$ scalar, and marginal densities $p_t$.
Write $\mathcal{L}_t\varphi = f\cdot\nabla\varphi + \tfrac12 g^2\Delta\varphi$ for the generator and
$k(x,s;\,y,t)$ for the transition density: the density of $X_t$ at $y$ given $X_s = x$, for $s<t$.
Everything below is stated for this state-independent isotropic $g$, which is what diffusion
models use; Anderson's paper handles general $\sigma(x,t)$ and we record that formula at the end.

Standing regularity assumption: $p_t$ and $k$ exist for $t>0$, are $C^2$ in the space variables
and $C^1$ in time, $p_t>0$, and decay fast enough at infinity for integration by parts. We also
assume the reverse SDE we construct has a unique law given its initial distribution (for instance
because its drift is locally Lipschitz). Haussmann and Pardoux give sharp conditions; for the OU
example below every assumption is verified because all densities are Gaussian.

From the [Fokker–Planck page](/concepts/fokker-planck), recall two facts about $k$:

- **Forward (Fokker–Planck) in $(y,t)$:** $\partial_t k = \mathcal{L}_t^{*}k$, and likewise $\partial_t p_t = \mathcal{L}_t^{*}p_t$.
- **Backward (Kolmogorov) in $(x,s)$:** $\partial_s k + \mathcal{L}_s k = 0$, because
  $k(x,s;y,t) = \mathbb{E}[\delta_y(X_t)\mid X_s = x]$ is an expectation of a terminal payoff.

The reversed process is $Y_s = X_{T-s}$, $0\le s\le T$. It starts at $Y_0 = X_T\sim p_T$ and ends at
$Y_T = X_0\sim p_0$. The question is which SDE it solves.

## The idea

<Intuition>
Reversing a process that only drifts is easy: flip the sign of the drift. Reversing diffusion is
not: noise spreads mass out going forward, and a plain sign flip of the drift would spread it out
further going backward instead of gathering it back. The correction that undoes the spreading is a
drift toward higher density, $g^2\nabla\log p_t$. Where the density is already low, this term
pulls the reversed process back toward where the data was.
</Intuition>

<Theorem name="Anderson's reverse-time SDE" label="Thm 1">
Under the standing assumptions, $Y_s = X_{T-s}$ is a weak solution of

$$
\mathrm{d}Y_s = \Big[-f(Y_s,T-s) + g(T-s)^2\,\nabla\log p_{T-s}(Y_s)\Big]\mathrm{d}s
+ g(T-s)\,\mathrm{d}\bar W_s , \qquad Y_0\sim p_T ,
$$

where $\bar W$ is a Brownian motion with respect to the filtration generated by $Y$. In the
forward clock, with $\bar W_t$ a Brownian motion running backward from $T$ and $\mathrm{d}t$ negative,
this is written $\mathrm{d}X_t = \big[f - g^2\nabla\log p_t\big]\mathrm{d}t + g\,\mathrm{d}\bar W_t$,
which is the form in <Cite id="2011.13456" />.
</Theorem>

Before the proof, here is the two-line calculation that papers usually present. It is a real
derivation of the *marginals* and it tells you what the answer must be, so it is worth seeing
first.

<Proposition name="Marginal matching" label="Prop 2">
Let $q_s = p_{T-s}$. Then $q$ solves the Fokker–Planck equation of the SDE in Thm 1. More
generally, for any $\lambda\ge 0$, $q$ solves the Fokker–Planck equation of

$$
\mathrm{d}Y_s = \Big[-f + \tfrac{1+\lambda^2}{2}\,g^2\,\nabla\log q_s\Big](Y_s,T-s)\,\mathrm{d}s + \lambda\,g(T-s)\,\mathrm{d}\bar W_s .
$$
</Proposition>

<Proof>
By the continuity-equation form of Fokker–Planck ([Prop 4 there](/concepts/fokker-planck)),
$\partial_t p_t = -\nabla\cdot\big(p_t(f - \tfrac12 g^2\nabla\log p_t)\big)$. Since
$\partial_s q_s = -(\partial_t p_t)|_{t = T-s}$,

$$
\partial_s q_s = \nabla\cdot\Big(q_s\big(f - \tfrac12 g^2\nabla\log q_s\big)\Big)
= -\nabla\cdot\Big(q_s\big(-f + \tfrac12 g^2\nabla\log q_s\big)\Big).
$$

An SDE with drift $\bar f$ and diffusion $\lambda g$ has Fokker–Planck equation
$\partial_s q = -\nabla\cdot\big(q(\bar f - \tfrac12\lambda^2 g^2\nabla\log q)\big)$ by the same
rewriting. Matching the two velocities,
$\bar f - \tfrac12\lambda^2 g^2\nabla\log q = -f + \tfrac12 g^2\nabla\log q$, gives
$\bar f = -f + \tfrac12(1+\lambda^2)g^2\nabla\log q$. Set $\lambda = 1$ for Thm 1. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Warning>
Prop 2 shows that the SDE in Thm 1 has the right one-time marginals $p_{T-s}$. It does not show
that it has the same *joint* law as $(X_{T-s})_s$: the whole $\lambda$-family has the same
marginals, and the members are different processes. Thm 1 is the statement that the $\lambda = 1$
member is the genuine time reversal. The proof needs the two-time structure, which is where the
backward equation enters.
</Warning>

<Proof>
(Of Thm 1.) The reversed process $Y$ is Markov, because the Markov property, conditional
independence of past and future given the present, is symmetric in time. So its law is determined
by $Y_0\sim p_T$ and its transition densities. By Bayes' rule, for $s<t$, the density of
$X_s$ at $x$ given $X_t = y$ is

$$
\bar k(y,t;\,x,s) = \frac{p_s(x)\,k(x,s;\,y,t)}{p_t(y)} .
$$

We show that, as a function of $(x,s)$ with $(y,t)$ fixed and time run backward, $\bar k$ solves
the Fokker–Planck equation of the SDE in Thm 1. Since $p_t(y)$ is a constant in this computation,
it is enough to treat $\rho(x,s) = p_s(x)\,k(x,s;y,t)$.

**Step 1: differentiate the product.** Using the forward equation for $p_s$ and the backward
equation for $k$,

$$
\partial_s\rho = k\,\partial_s p + p\,\partial_s k = k\,\mathcal{L}_s^{*}p - p\,\mathcal{L}_s k .
$$

**Step 2: expand.** With $\mathcal{L}^{*}p = -\nabla\cdot(fp) + \tfrac12 g^2\Delta p$ and
$\mathcal{L}k = f\cdot\nabla k + \tfrac12 g^2\Delta k$,

$$
k\,\mathcal{L}^{*}p - p\,\mathcal{L}k
= -\big[k\,\nabla\cdot(fp) + p\,f\cdot\nabla k\big] + \tfrac12 g^2\big[k\,\Delta p - p\,\Delta k\big]
= -\nabla\cdot(f\,pk) + \tfrac12 g^2\,\nabla\cdot\big(k\nabla p - p\nabla k\big),
$$

where the first bracket is the product rule and the second is the identity
$\nabla\cdot(k\nabla p - p\nabla k) = k\Delta p - p\Delta k$ (the cross terms $\nabla k\cdot\nabla p$ cancel).

**Step 3: introduce the score.** Write $k\nabla p - p\nabla k = 2k\nabla p - \nabla(pk) = 2\,pk\,\nabla\log p - \nabla(pk)$.
Substituting, and remembering $\rho = pk$,

$$
\partial_s\rho = -\nabla\cdot\Big(\big(f - g^2\nabla\log p_s\big)\rho\Big) - \tfrac12 g^2\Delta\rho .
$$

**Step 4: reverse the clock.** Put $\sigma = T - s$ (so $\sigma$ increases as $s$ decreases) and
$\bar f(x,\sigma) = -f(x,T-\sigma) + g(T-\sigma)^2\nabla\log p_{T-\sigma}(x)$. Then
$\partial_\sigma\rho = -\partial_s\rho$ and the equation becomes

$$
\partial_\sigma\rho = -\nabla\cdot\big(\bar f\,\rho\big) + \tfrac12 g(T-\sigma)^2\,\Delta\rho ,
$$

which is the Fokker–Planck equation of $\mathrm{d}Y = \bar f\,\mathrm{d}\sigma + g\,\mathrm{d}\bar W$.
Dividing by $p_t(y)$, the reverse transition density $\bar k$ solves the same equation in
$(x,\sigma)$, with initial condition $\bar k\to\delta_y$ as $s\uparrow t$ (i.e. as $\sigma$ decreases
to $T-t$).

**Step 5: conclude.** The Fokker–Planck equation with a point initial condition and a unique
solution is solved by the transition density of the SDE with those coefficients. So the Markov
process $Y$ has the transition densities of the SDE in Thm 1 and the initial law $p_T$; its
finite-dimensional distributions coincide with those of that SDE's solution, and therefore $Y$ is
a weak solution of it. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

What was skipped: that $\bar k$ is the *unique* solution of the reverse Fokker–Planck equation with
initial condition $\delta_y$, and that matching finite-dimensional distributions upgrades to
"$Y$ is a weak solution" (this needs the reverse SDE to have a unique law, which we assumed).
Haussmann and Pardoux prove the theorem under the assumption that $f$ is Lipschitz and
$\nabla\log p_t$ is locally integrable in a suitable sense, avoiding smooth densities.

<Corollary name="Probability flow ODE" label="Cor 3">
The deterministic flow

$$
\dot x_t = f(x_t,t) - \tfrac12 g(t)^2\,\nabla\log p_t(x_t), \qquad x_0\sim p_0,
$$

has marginals $p_t$ for all $t$: running it backward from $x_T\sim p_T$ produces samples from $p_0$.
</Corollary>

<Proof>
This is Prop 2 with $\lambda = 0$ read in the forward clock: the velocity
$v_t = f - \tfrac12 g^2\nabla\log p_t$ satisfies the [continuity equation](/concepts/continuity-equation)
$\partial_t p_t + \nabla\cdot(p_t v_t) = 0$, and a smooth velocity field transports the density
$p_0$ along its characteristics to the solution of that equation, which is $p_t$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

The ODE and the SDE agree on every $p_t$ and on nothing else: the ODE couples $x_0$ and $x_T$
deterministically, the SDE does not. The ODE gives exact likelihoods through the instantaneous
change of variables and is what DDIM and higher-order samplers discretise; the SDE ($\lambda = 1$)
and the intermediate $\lambda$'s of <Cite id="2206.00364" /> trade determinism for error correction
from fresh noise.

**General $\sigma$.** For $\mathrm{d}X = f\,\mathrm{d}t + \sigma(X_t,t)\,\mathrm{d}W$ with
$D = \sigma\sigma^{\top}$, Anderson's drift is
$\bar f_i = -f_i + p_t^{-1}\sum_j\partial_j(D_{ij}\,p_t) = -f_i + \sum_j\partial_j D_{ij} + (D\nabla\log p_t)_i$,
with the same $\sigma$ in front of $\mathrm{d}\bar W$. For $\sigma = gI$ the middle term vanishes
and we recover Thm 1.

## Worked example by hand

<Example title="OU forward process with Gaussian data, reversed exactly">
Take $d=1$, $f(x,t) = -x$, $g = \sqrt2$ (the OU process with unit stationary variance), and
Gaussian data $p_0 = \mathcal{N}(0, 4)$, wider than the stationary $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$. Horizon $T = \ln 2$.

**Forward marginals.** From the [OU page](/concepts/linear-sde-and-ou), $p_t = \mathcal{N}(0, v_t)$
with $\dot v = -2v + 2$, $v_0 = 4$, so

$$
v_t = 1 + 3e^{-2t}, \qquad v_T = 1 + \tfrac34 = \tfrac74 .
$$

**Score.** $\nabla\log p_t(x) = -x/v_t$. At $t = T$ and $x = 1$: $-4/7$.

**Reverse SDE (Thm 1).** With $q_s = p_{T-s}$,

$$
\mathrm{d}Y_s = \Big[\,Y_s + 2\cdot\Big(-\frac{Y_s}{v_{T-s}}\Big)\Big]\mathrm{d}s + \sqrt2\,\mathrm{d}\bar W_s
= a(s)\,Y_s\,\mathrm{d}s + \sqrt2\,\mathrm{d}\bar W_s,
\qquad a(s) = 1 - \frac{2}{v_{T-s}},
\qquad Y_0\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tfrac74).
$$

At $s = 0$: $a(0) = 1 - 8/7 = -1/7$. The reverse drift is *contracting* even though the
reversed process must spread from variance $7/4$ to variance $4$: the spreading is done by the
noise, and the score term only tempers it. Check with the variance ODE of a linear SDE,
$\dot w = 2a(s)\,w + 2$: at $s=0$, $\dot w = 2(-\tfrac17)(\tfrac74) + 2 = -\tfrac12 + 2 = \tfrac32 > 0$.
Meanwhile forward, $\dot v_T = -2\cdot\tfrac74 + 2 = -\tfrac32$. Equal and opposite, as they must be.

**Full check of the marginals.** Claim: $w_s = v_{T-s}$ solves $\dot w = 2a(s)w + 2$. Left side:
$\dot w_s = -\dot v_{T-s} = 2v_{T-s} - 2$. Right side:
$2\big(1 - \tfrac{2}{v_{T-s}}\big)v_{T-s} + 2 = 2v_{T-s} - 4 + 2 = 2v_{T-s} - 2$. Same. The mean stays
at $0$. So $Y_s\sim\mathcal{N}(0, v_{T-s})$ and $Y_T\sim\mathcal{N}(0,4) = p_0$: the reverse SDE
lands exactly on the data distribution.

**Probability flow ODE (Cor 3).**

$$
\dot x = -x - \tfrac12\cdot 2\cdot\Big(-\frac{x}{v_t}\Big) = -\Big(1 - \frac1{v_t}\Big)x .
$$

Guess $x_t = \sqrt{v_t/v_0}\;x_0$, a pure rescaling. Check: $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log x_t = \dot v_t/(2v_t) = (-2v_t+2)/(2v_t) = -(1 - 1/v_t)$.
Correct. So the flow map from $p_0$ to $p_T$ is $x\mapsto\sqrt{7/16}\,x\approx 0.661\,x$: the
point $x_0 = 2$ (one standard deviation of $p_0$) goes to $x_T = \sqrt7/2\approx 1.323$, which is
one standard deviation of $p_T = \mathcal{N}(0,7/4)$. Quantiles are preserved, as they must be
for a monotone map between centred Gaussians. This is also the optimal transport map between
them (see [Wasserstein distance](/concepts/wasserstein-distance)), a coincidence special to
Gaussians with linear drift.

**One Euler–Maruyama step of the sampler.** Start at $y_0 = 1.323$, step $\Delta s = 0.1$, draw
$\xi = 0.3$ from $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$:

$$
y_{0.1} = y_0 + a(0)\,y_0\,\Delta s + \sqrt2\sqrt{\Delta s}\,\xi
= 1.323 - 0.0189 + 0.4243\cdot 0.3 \approx 1.431 .
$$

**DDPM dictionary.** With $f = -\tfrac12\beta x$, $g = \sqrt\beta$ this is the DDPM forward process,
$x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon$, and the score of the perturbation
kernel is $\nabla\log p_t(x_t\mid x_0) = -\epsilon/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$. Tweedie's formula
([score matching](/concepts/score-matching)) gives $\nabla\log p_t(x_t) = -\mathbb{E}[\epsilon\mid x_t]/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$,
so the $\epsilon$-prediction network of DDPM is $-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$ times the score, and
DDPM's ancestral sampler is Euler–Maruyama on Thm 1 (the appendices of <Cite id="2011.13456" />).
</Example>

## Where it shows up

- **Score SDE.** Eq. (6) of <Cite id="2011.13456" /> is Thm 1; Eq. (13) is Cor 3; the appendices show
  DDPM ancestral sampling and SMLD are discretisations of Thm 1, and Section 4.3 uses Cor 3 for
  exact likelihoods via the instantaneous change of variables from the
  [continuity equation](/concepts/continuity-equation). The full framework is on the
  [diffusion SDE](/concepts/diffusion-sde-framework) page.
- **EDM and DDIM.** Eq. (6) of <Cite id="2206.00364" /> is exactly the $\lambda$-family of Prop 2
  with $\beta(t)$ controlling how much fresh noise to inject; DDIM with $\eta = 0$ is a first-order
  integrator of Cor 3 in a rescaled parametrisation.
- **Diffusion Policy.** <Cite id="2303.04137" /> samples an action chunk by running $K$ DDPM (or
  DDIM) steps of the reverse process with a score conditioned on the observation; the conditioning
  changes nothing in Thm 1 because it only changes $p_0$.
- **Flow matching and $\pi_0$.** The velocity in Cor 3 is the marginal vector field of
  [flow matching](/concepts/flow-matching) for the Gaussian diffusion path, which is why
  score-based and flow-based samplers are interchangeable at the level of marginals; $\pi_0$ trains
  the ODE side directly.
- **Schrödinger bridges.** Each IPF half-step in De Bortoli et al. 2021 takes the current process,
  time-reverses it with Thm 1, and fits the reversed drift by regression; see
  [Schrödinger bridge](/concepts/schrodinger-bridge).

## Going deeper

Anderson's original paper derives Thm 1 via the joint density argument above, for general
$\sigma(x,t)$, and also treats the reverse-time Kalman–Bucy filter, which is where the result came
from. Haussmann and Pardoux prove it under minimal regularity using the martingale problem and
an integration-by-parts argument that avoids smooth transition densities altogether; that is the
version cited for non-smooth data distributions. Föllmer's 1985 note *An entropy approach to the
time reversal of diffusion processes* obtains the same drift from a relative-entropy argument
that connects directly to the [Girsanov](/concepts/girsanov-theorem) and
[Schrödinger bridge](/concepts/schrodinger-bridge) pages.
