## Why you need this

Hyvärinen's score matching objective, Theorem 1 of his 2005 paper, replaces the unknown
$\nabla\log p$ in $\mathbb{E}_p\|s_\theta - \nabla\log p\|^2$ by a divergence term
$\mathbb{E}_p[\nabla\cdot s_\theta]$; the step is one integration by parts. The Fokker–Planck
equation, from which Song et al. 2021 derive the probability-flow ODE and Anderson's reverse
SDE, is the adjoint of the generator of an SDE, computed by integrating by parts twice. Stein's
identity $\mathbb{E}[(Z-\mu)f(Z)] = \Sigma\,\mathbb{E}[\nabla f(Z)]$ is the Gaussian case; it
is the engine of Stein variational gradient descent, of Stein's unbiased risk estimate for
training denoisers, and of the equality between the reparameterization gradient and the
score-function gradient of a Gaussian policy. This page proves the identity once in the form
that covers all of these.

## Setup

For $f:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}$ and $v:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}^d$ continuously
differentiable, $\nabla f$ is the gradient, $\nabla\cdot v = \sum_i\partial_i v_i$ the
divergence, $J_v$ the Jacobian, and $\Delta f = \nabla\cdot\nabla f$ the Laplacian. The product
rule

$$
\nabla\cdot(f v) = \nabla f\cdot v + f\,\nabla\cdot v
$$

is the whole algebra. A density $p$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$ is assumed $C^1$ and positive, and its
score is $\nabla\log p = \nabla p/p$. We write $S_R = \{x : \|x\| = R\}$ for the sphere of
radius $R$ and $\mathrm{d}S$ for surface measure on it; $\|S_R\| = c_d R^{d-1}$.

## The idea

<Theorem name="Divergence theorem" label="Thm 1">
Let $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^d$ be bounded with piecewise $C^1$ boundary $\partial\Omega$ and
outward unit normal $n$, and let $F:\overline\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^d$ be $C^1$. Then
$$
\int_\Omega\nabla\cdot F\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_{\partial\Omega}F\cdot n\,\mathrm{d}S .
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
We prove it for a rectangle $\Omega = [a,b]\times[c,d]$ in $d = 2$, which shows the mechanism;
Evans Appendix C.2 handles general domains by covering $\partial\Omega$ with patches that look
like graphs and adding up. By Fubini and the fundamental theorem of calculus,

$$
\int_\Omega\partial_x F_1\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y = \int_c^d\big(F_1(b,y) - F_1(a,y)\big)\mathrm{d}y ,
$$

which is the flux of $F$ through the right side (normal $+e_1$) plus the flux through the
left side (normal $-e_1$). The same computation for $\partial_y F_2$ gives the flux through the
top and bottom. Adding the two gives the boundary integral over all four sides. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Corollary name="Integration by parts on all of R^d" label="Cor 2">
Let $F:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}^d$ be $C^1$ with $\nabla\cdot F$ integrable and
$\int_{S_R}\|F\|\,\mathrm{d}S\to 0$ as $R\to\infty$. Then $\int_{\mathbb{R}^d}\nabla\cdot F\,\mathrm{d}x = 0$.
Consequently, for $C^1$ functions $f$ (scalar) and $v$ (vector) and a $C^1$ density $p$ such
that $F = f v p$ satisfies the hypotheses,

$$
\mathbb{E}_p[\nabla f\cdot v] = -\,\mathbb{E}_p[f\,\nabla\cdot v] - \mathbb{E}_p[f\,v\cdot\nabla\log p] .
$$

A sufficient condition: $f$, $\nabla f$, $v$, $J_v$ have polynomial growth and $p$ has
Gaussian-type tails, $p(x)\le Ce^{-c\|x\|^2}$ with $\nabla p$ of the same order.
</Corollary>

<Proof>
Apply Thm 1 on the ball $\|x\|\le R$: $\big|\int_{\|x\|\le R}\nabla\cdot F\,\mathrm{d}x\big|
= \big|\int_{S_R}F\cdot n\,\mathrm{d}S\big|\le\int_{S_R}\|F\|\,\mathrm{d}S\to 0$. The left side
converges to $\int_{\mathbb{R}^d}\nabla\cdot F$ by dominated convergence (dominator $|\nabla\cdot F|$).
For the second claim expand $\nabla\cdot(f v p) = (\nabla f\cdot v)p + f(\nabla\cdot v)p + f\,v\cdot\nabla p$
and write $\nabla p = p\,\nabla\log p$. Under the sufficient condition,
$\|F\|\le C(1+\|x\|^k)e^{-c\|x\|^2}$ on $S_R$, so $\int_{S_R}\|F\|\,\mathrm{d}S\le C'R^{d-1+k}e^{-cR^2}\to 0$,
and $\nabla\cdot F$ is integrable for the same reason. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Intuition>
On a bounded domain, integration by parts trades a derivative on $f$ for a derivative on
everything else at the cost of a boundary term. On $\mathbb{R}^d$ the "boundary" is a sphere
at infinity, and the boundary term vanishes exactly when the density kills the integrand out
there. The three terms of Cor 2 are the three places the derivative can land: on $f$, on $v$,
or on $p$, and the last one produces the score. That is the entire reason scores appear in
score matching and in Fokker–Planck.
</Intuition>

<Theorem name="Stein's identity (Gaussian integration by parts)" label="Thm 3">
Let $Z\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma)$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$ with $\Sigma\succ 0$, and let
$f:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}$ be $C^1$ with $f$ and $\nabla f$ of polynomial growth. Then
$$
\mathbb{E}\big[(Z-\mu)f(Z)\big] = \Sigma\,\mathbb{E}\big[\nabla f(Z)\big] .
$$
In one dimension with $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$: $\mathbb{E}[Zf(Z)] = \mathbb{E}[f'(Z)]$.
Conversely, if $Z$ is a real random variable with $\mathbb{E}[f'(Z) - Zf(Z)] = 0$ for every
bounded $f$ with bounded continuous derivative, then $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Take $v = e_i$ (constant, so $\nabla\cdot v = 0$) in Cor 2, with $p$ the Gaussian density and
$\nabla\log p(z) = -\Sigma^{-1}(z-\mu)$ (see
[Gaussian computations](/concepts/gaussian-computations), Prop 4). The hypotheses of Cor 2 hold
by its sufficient condition. Then

$$
\mathbb{E}[\partial_i f(Z)] = -\mathbb{E}\big[f(Z)\,\partial_i\log p(Z)\big]
= \mathbb{E}\big[f(Z)\,(\Sigma^{-1}(Z-\mu))_i\big] .
$$

Stacking over $i$: $\mathbb{E}[\nabla f(Z)] = \Sigma^{-1}\mathbb{E}[(Z-\mu)f(Z)]$; multiply by
$\Sigma$. In one dimension the same thing is visible directly: $z\varphi(z) = -\varphi'(z)$ for
the standard normal density, so $\mathbb{E}[Zf(Z)] = -\int f\varphi' = \big[-f\varphi\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + \int f'\varphi = \mathbb{E}[f'(Z)]$,
the boundary term vanishing because $f$ grows polynomially.

For the converse we only sketch: given a bounded continuous $h$, the ODE
$f'(z) - zf(z) = h(z) - \mathbb{E}[h(N)]$ with $N\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ has the bounded solution
$f(z) = e^{z^2/2}\int_{-\infty}^z(h(u) - \mathbb{E}h(N))e^{-u^2/2}\mathrm{d}u$ with bounded
derivative. Plugging it into the hypothesis gives $\mathbb{E}[h(Z)] = \mathbb{E}[h(N)]$ for all
such $h$, which determines the law. The skipped step is checking the boundedness of $f$ and
$f'$, a calculus estimate on Gaussian tails. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Proposition name="The score matching identity" label="Prop 4">
Let $p$ be a $C^1$ density and $s:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}^d$ a $C^1$ vector field such that
$F = s\,p$ satisfies the hypotheses of Cor 2 and $\mathbb{E}_p\|s\|^2$, $\mathbb{E}_p\|\nabla\log p\|^2$
are finite. Then
$$
\mathbb{E}_p\Big[\tfrac12\|s - \nabla\log p\|^2\Big]
= \mathbb{E}_p\Big[\tfrac12\|s\|^2 + \nabla\cdot s\Big] + \tfrac12\,\mathbb{E}_p\|\nabla\log p\|^2 .
$$
The right side can be estimated from samples of $p$ without knowing $p$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Expand $\tfrac12\|s - \nabla\log p\|^2 = \tfrac12\|s\|^2 - s\cdot\nabla\log p + \tfrac12\|\nabla\log p\|^2$.
For the cross term use Cor 2 with $f\equiv 1$ and $v = s$:
$\mathbb{E}_p[s\cdot\nabla\log p] = -\mathbb{E}_p[\nabla\cdot s]$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Proposition name="Adjoint of the generator (the Fokker–Planck operator)" label="Prop 5">
For an SDE $\mathrm{d}X = b(x)\,\mathrm{d}t + \sigma(x)\,\mathrm{d}W$ with $a = \sigma\sigma^\top$,
the generator is $Lf = b\cdot\nabla f + \tfrac12\sum_{ij}a_{ij}\partial_i\partial_j f$. For
$f\in C^2$ with compact support and $p\in C^2$,
$$
\int (Lf)\,p\,\mathrm{d}x = \int f\,(L^*p)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad
L^*p = -\nabla\cdot(bp) + \tfrac12\sum_{ij}\partial_i\partial_j(a_{ij}p) .
$$
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Compact support makes every boundary term vanish, so Cor 2 applies with no decay condition.
Drift term: $\int(b\cdot\nabla f)p = \int\nabla f\cdot(bp) = -\int f\,\nabla\cdot(bp)$ (Cor 2
with $F = f\,bp$, whose divergence is $\nabla f\cdot bp + f\nabla\cdot(bp)$). Diffusion term:
$\int a_{ij}p\,\partial_i\partial_j f = -\int\partial_i(a_{ij}p)\,\partial_j f = \int f\,\partial_j\partial_i(a_{ij}p)$,
integrating by parts once in $x_i$ and once in $x_j$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

This is the identity behind the [Fokker–Planck equation](/concepts/fokker-planck): Itô's lemma
and the martingale property of the stochastic integral give
$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[f(X_t)] = \mathbb{E}[(Lf)(X_t)]$, i.e.
$\int f\,\partial_t p_t = \int (Lf)\,p_t = \int f\,L^*p_t$ for every test $f$, hence
$\partial_t p_t = L^*p_t$.

<Warning>
The identity fails without the decay hypothesis, and the failure is exactly the boundary term.
Take $p$ uniform on $[0,1]$ and $s(x) = x$. Inside the interval $\nabla\log p = 0$, so the
"cross term" $\mathbb{E}_p[s\,\partial_x\log p]$ is $0$, while $-\mathbb{E}_p[s'] = -1$. The
missing $1$ is $\big[x\cdot p(x)\big]_0^1 = 1$. Densities with jumps, bounded supports, or
heavy tails against fast-growing $s$ all need the boundary term checked.
</Warning>

## Worked example by hand

<Example title="Stein's identity checked, then used">
$Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$. With $f(z) = z^3$: the left side is $\mathbb{E}[Z^4] = 3$, the right
side is $\mathbb{E}[3Z^2] = 3$. With $f(z) = e^{az}$: the left side is
$\mathbb{E}[Ze^{aZ}] = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}a}\mathbb{E}[e^{aZ}] = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}a}e^{a^2/2} = ae^{a^2/2}$,
the right side is $\mathbb{E}[ae^{aZ}] = ae^{a^2/2}$. Now use it: with $f(z) = z^{n}$,
$\mathbb{E}[Z^{n+1}] = n\,\mathbb{E}[Z^{n-1}]$, so $\mathbb{E}Z^2 = 1$, $\mathbb{E}Z^4 = 3$,
$\mathbb{E}Z^6 = 15$, $\mathbb{E}Z^8 = 105$: Wick's formula for the one-dimensional case in a
single line.

In two dimensions take $\mu = 0$, $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}4 & 2\\ 2 & 3\end{pmatrix}$ and
$f(z) = z_1^3$. Left side: $\mathbb{E}[Zz_1^3] = (\mathbb{E}Z_1^4,\ \mathbb{E}Z_1^3Z_2)^\top
= (3\Sigma_{11}^2,\ 3\Sigma_{11}\Sigma_{12})^\top = (48, 24)^\top$ by Wick. Right side:
$\nabla f = (3z_1^2, 0)^\top$, $\mathbb{E}\nabla f = (12, 0)^\top$, and
$\Sigma(12,0)^\top = (48, 24)^\top$. Agreed. The second component is the interesting one:
the identity produces the cross moment $\mathbb{E}Z_1^3Z_2$ from a derivative that does not
involve $z_2$ at all, because $\Sigma$ carries the correlation.
</Example>

<Example title="Score matching for a one-parameter Gaussian family">
Let $p = \mathcal N(0,1)$, so $\nabla\log p(x) = -x$, and fit $s_\theta(x) = -\theta x$.
Directly: $\tfrac12\mathbb{E}_p(-\theta x + x)^2 = \tfrac12(1-\theta)^2\mathbb{E}x^2 = \tfrac12(1-\theta)^2$.
Hyvärinen's form: $\tfrac12\mathbb{E}_p[\theta^2x^2] + \mathbb{E}_p[\partial_x s_\theta] = \tfrac12\theta^2 - \theta$.
The difference is $\tfrac12(1-\theta)^2 - \tfrac12\theta^2 + \theta = \tfrac12$, which is
$\tfrac12\mathbb{E}_p\|\nabla\log p\|^2 = \tfrac12\mathbb{E}x^2$ as Prop 4 predicts, and it
does not depend on $\theta$. Both expressions are minimized at $\theta = 1$, where
$s_\theta = \nabla\log p$. With samples $x_1,\dots,x_n$ from $p$, the estimator
$\frac1n\sum_k(\tfrac12\theta^2x_k^2 - \theta)$ is minimized at $\theta = n/\sum_k x_k^2$, the
inverse of the empirical second moment, which is the maximum likelihood estimate of a
precision. Score matching found it without ever normalizing a density.
</Example>

<Example title="The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck adjoint and its stationary density">
$\mathrm{d}X = -X\,\mathrm{d}t + \sqrt2\,\mathrm{d}W$ in one dimension: $b = -x$, $a = 2$,
so $Lf = -xf' + f''$ and by Prop 5 $L^*p = (xp)' + p''$. Check the standard normal
$\varphi$ is stationary: $(x\varphi)' = \varphi + x\varphi' = \varphi - x^2\varphi$ and
$\varphi'' = (-x\varphi)' = -\varphi + x^2\varphi$, which sum to $0$. Check Prop 5 itself with
$f = x^2$: $Lf = -2x^2 + 2$, so $\int(Lf)\varphi = -2 + 2 = 0$, and $\int f\,L^*\varphi = 0$
since $L^*\varphi = 0$. Consistent: at stationarity the second moment does not change.
</Example>

## Where it shows up

- Hyvärinen 2005, Theorem 1, is Prop 4; the paper's Appendix gives the same proof with the
  decay condition stated as $p(x)s(x)\to 0$ at infinity. Sliced score matching (Song et al.
  2019) estimates $\nabla\cdot s$ by $\mathbb{E}_v[v^\top J_s v]$ with random $v$; denoising
  score matching avoids the divergence altogether. See
  [score matching](/concepts/score-matching).
- Song et al., <Cite id="2011.13456" />, Appendix A, derive the probability-flow ODE from the
  Fokker–Planck equation, which is Prop 5 applied to the forward SDE; Anderson's reverse-time
  SDE on [time reversal](/concepts/time-reversal-of-diffusions) comes from the same identity
  applied in reversed time. -
SVGD, <Cite id="1608.04471" />: the Stein operator $\mathcal A_p\phi = \phi\cdot\nabla\log p + \nabla\cdot\phi$
  satisfies $\mathbb{E}_p[\mathcal A_p\phi] = 0$, which is Cor 2 with $f\equiv 1$; the kernelized
  Stein discrepancy measures how badly a sample set violates it, and the SVGD update is the
  direction of steepest violation. -
Stein's unbiased risk estimate: for $Y = X + \sigma\varepsilon$ and a denoiser $f$,
  $\mathbb{E}\|f(Y) - X\|^2 = \mathbb{E}\|f(Y) - Y\|^2 + 2\sigma^2\mathbb{E}[\nabla\cdot f(Y)] - d\sigma^2$
  (Exercise 3), so denoisers can be trained without clean targets. -
Policy gradients: for $A = \mu + \sigma\varepsilon$ the pathwise gradient
  $\nabla_\mu\mathbb{E}[r(A)] = \mathbb{E}[\nabla r(A)]$ and the score-function gradient
  $\mathbb{E}[r(A)(A-\mu)/\sigma^2]$ are equal by Thm 3. SAC uses the first, REINFORCE the
  second; the [policy gradient theorem](/concepts/policy-gradient-theorem) page compares
  their variances.

## Going deeper

Evans, Appendix C.2, has the divergence theorem for $C^1$ domains and the standard
integration-by-parts formulas that follow. Stein's 1981 paper introduces the identity for the
multivariate normal and proves the unbiased risk estimate. For the characterization direction
of Thm 3 and its use in proving central limit theorems, see Ross, *Fundamentals of Stein's
Method* (Probability Surveys, 2011), Section 2. Hyvärinen's paper is short and worth reading
in full; its Theorem 1 and Appendix A are exactly Prop 4.
