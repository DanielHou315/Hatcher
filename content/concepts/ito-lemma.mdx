## Why you need this

Every diffusion model paper writes a forward process as an SDE,
$\mathrm{d}X_t = f(X_t,t)\,\mathrm{d}t + g(t)\,\mathrm{d}W_t$, and then reasons about
$\log p_t(X_t)$, $\|X_t\|^2$, or $e^{-\int \beta}X_t$ as if they were ordinary functions of
$X_t$. They are not: $X_t$ has infinite variation, so the classical chain rule fails. Itô's lemma
is the corrected chain rule. It is the single computation behind the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck
closed form used in DDPM, the derivation of the Fokker–Planck equation, and the
time-reversal formula of Anderson that makes score-based sampling work.

## Setup

Let $W_t$ be a standard Brownian motion and let $X_t$ solve

$$
\mathrm{d}X_t = \mu(X_t,t)\,\mathrm{d}t + \sigma(X_t,t)\,\mathrm{d}W_t ,
$$

which is shorthand for $X_t = X_0 + \int_0^t \mu\,\mathrm{d}s + \int_0^t \sigma\,\mathrm{d}W_s$
with the second integral an [Itô integral](/concepts/stochastic-integral). Let
$f:\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}$ be $C^{2,1}$: twice continuously differentiable in
$x$, once in $t$.

<Definition name="Quadratic variation">
For a process $Y$, the quadratic variation on $[0,t]$ is the limit in probability of
$\sum_k (Y_{t_{k+1}}-Y_{t_k})^2$ over partitions whose mesh goes to zero. For Brownian motion
$[W]_t = t$. For a smooth function, $[Y]_t = 0$. This is the whole story: Brownian increments
are of size $\sqrt{\mathrm{d}t}$, so their squares are of size $\mathrm{d}t$ and cannot be
dropped.
</Definition>

## The idea

<Theorem name="Itô's lemma (one dimension)" label="Thm 1">
With $X$ and $f$ as above,
$$
\mathrm{d}f(X_t,t)
= \Big( \partial_t f + \mu\,\partial_x f + \tfrac12 \sigma^2\,\partial_{xx} f \Big)\mathrm{d}t
+ \sigma\,\partial_x f\,\mathrm{d}W_t ,
$$
where every partial derivative is evaluated at $(X_t,t)$.
</Theorem>

<Intuition>
Taylor-expand $f$ to second order and keep the rules
$\mathrm{d}t\cdot\mathrm{d}t = 0$, $\mathrm{d}t\cdot\mathrm{d}W_t = 0$,
$\mathrm{d}W_t\cdot\mathrm{d}W_t = \mathrm{d}t$. The last rule is quadratic variation. The
extra $\tfrac12\sigma^2 \partial_{xx}f$ term is called the Itô correction.
</Intuition>

<Proof>
Fix $t$ and a partition $0 = t_0 < \dots < t_n = t$ with mesh $\to 0$. Write
$\Delta X_k = X_{t_{k+1}} - X_{t_k}$ and $\Delta t_k = t_{k+1}-t_k$. Taylor's theorem with
remainder gives, for some point on the segment,

$$
f(X_{t_{k+1}},t_{k+1}) - f(X_{t_k},t_k)
= \partial_t f\,\Delta t_k + \partial_x f\,\Delta X_k + \tfrac12 \partial_{xx} f\,(\Delta X_k)^2
+ R_k ,
$$

where $R_k$ collects terms of order $\Delta t_k\,\Delta X_k$, $(\Delta t_k)^2$, and
$o((\Delta X_k)^2)$. Sum over $k$ and send the mesh to zero.

1. $\sum_k \partial_t f\,\Delta t_k \to \int_0^t \partial_t f\,\mathrm{d}s$ (Riemann sum).
2. $\sum_k \partial_x f\,\Delta X_k \to \int_0^t \partial_x f\,\mathrm{d}X_s
   = \int_0^t \partial_x f\,\mu\,\mathrm{d}s + \int_0^t \partial_x f\,\sigma\,\mathrm{d}W_s$,
   by definition of the Itô integral (the integrand is evaluated at the left endpoint, which is
   exactly what Taylor's expansion around $t_k$ produced).
3. $(\Delta X_k)^2 = \sigma^2 (\Delta W_k)^2 + 2\mu\sigma\,\Delta t_k \Delta W_k + \mu^2(\Delta t_k)^2$.
   The last two terms are $o(\Delta t_k)$ and their sums vanish. For the first,
   $\sum_k \partial_{xx} f\,\sigma^2 (\Delta W_k)^2 \to \int_0^t \partial_{xx} f\,\sigma^2\,\mathrm{d}s$
   because $\sum_k h(t_k)(\Delta W_k)^2 \to \int h\,\mathrm{d}s$ in $L^2$ for continuous
   adapted $h$: the summands have mean $h(t_k)\Delta t_k$ and variance $2h(t_k)^2(\Delta t_k)^2$,
   and the sum of variances is $O(\text{mesh})\to 0$.
4. $\sum_k R_k \to 0$ by the same size counting.

Collecting the limits gives the integral form of the statement. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Theorem name="Itô's lemma (vector form)" label="Thm 2">
If $X_t\in\mathbb{R}^d$ solves $\mathrm{d}X_t = \mu\,\mathrm{d}t + \Sigma\,\mathrm{d}W_t$ with
$W$ an $m$-dimensional Brownian motion and $\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}$, then for
$f\in C^{2,1}(\mathbb{R}^d\times[0,\infty))$
$$
\mathrm{d}f = \Big(\partial_t f + \nabla f^{\top}\mu
+ \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}\!\big(\Sigma\Sigma^{\top}\nabla^2 f\big)\Big)\mathrm{d}t
+ \nabla f^{\top}\Sigma\,\mathrm{d}W_t .
$$
</Theorem>

The trace term is the only new thing: the product rule
$\mathrm{d}W^{(i)}\mathrm{d}W^{(j)} = \delta_{ij}\,\mathrm{d}t$ turns the quadratic form
$\tfrac12\,\mathrm{d}X^{\top}\nabla^2 f\,\mathrm{d}X$ into $\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma\Sigma^\top \nabla^2 f)\,\mathrm{d}t$.

<Warning>
The correction term has the sign and factor $+\tfrac12\sigma^2 f''$ for an Itô integral. If
you see $\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_t$ (Stratonovich), the classical chain rule holds and the
correction is absorbed into the drift. Diffusion-model papers use Itô unless they say otherwise.
</Warning>

## Worked example by hand

<Example title="Geometric Brownian motion">
Let $\mathrm{d}X_t = a X_t\,\mathrm{d}t + b X_t\,\mathrm{d}W_t$ with $X_0 = 1$, $a = 0.1$,
$b = 0.3$. Guess: $X_t$ should be an exponential. Apply Itô to $f(x) = \log x$, so
$\partial_x f = 1/x$ and $\partial_{xx} f = -1/x^2$, and $\partial_t f = 0$.

Plug into Thm 1 with $\mu = ax$, $\sigma = bx$:

$$
\mathrm{d}\log X_t
= \Big( ax\cdot\tfrac1x + \tfrac12 b^2x^2\cdot\big(-\tfrac{1}{x^2}\big) \Big)\mathrm{d}t
+ bx\cdot\tfrac1x\,\mathrm{d}W_t
= \big(a - \tfrac12 b^2\big)\mathrm{d}t + b\,\mathrm{d}W_t .
$$

The right-hand side has constant coefficients, so integrate directly:

$$
\log X_t = \big(a - \tfrac12 b^2\big)t + bW_t ,
\qquad
X_t = \exp\!\Big(\big(a-\tfrac12 b^2\big)t + bW_t\Big).
$$

With the numbers: $a - \tfrac12 b^2 = 0.1 - 0.045 = 0.055$. At $t = 1$ on a path with
$W_1 = 0.5$, $X_1 = e^{0.055 + 0.15} = e^{0.205} \approx 1.228$.

Check the mean. Since $W_1 \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$ and $\mathbb{E}[e^{bW_1}] = e^{b^2/2}$,
$$
\mathbb{E}[X_1] = e^{0.055}\,e^{0.045} = e^{0.1} = e^{a}.
$$
That is what you get from the drift alone, as it should be: the Itô correction in the
exponent exactly cancels the Jensen gap $\mathbb{E}[e^{bW}] > e^{\mathbb{E}[bW]}$. Without the
correction you would have predicted $\mathbb{E}[X_1] = e^{0.145}$, wrong by 4.6%.
</Example>

<Example title="The DDPM forward process is an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck SDE">
Take $\mathrm{d}X_t = -\tfrac12\beta X_t\,\mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{\beta}\,\mathrm{d}W_t$ with
constant $\beta$. Apply Itô to $f(x,t) = e^{\beta t/2}x$:
$\partial_t f = \tfrac{\beta}{2}e^{\beta t/2}x$, $\partial_x f = e^{\beta t/2}$,
$\partial_{xx} f = 0$. Then

$$
\mathrm{d}\big(e^{\beta t/2}X_t\big)
= \Big(\tfrac{\beta}{2}e^{\beta t/2}X_t - \tfrac{\beta}{2}e^{\beta t/2}X_t\Big)\mathrm{d}t
+ e^{\beta t/2}\sqrt{\beta}\,\mathrm{d}W_t
= \sqrt{\beta}\,e^{\beta t/2}\,\mathrm{d}W_t .
$$

Integrate and divide: $X_t = e^{-\beta t/2}X_0 + \sqrt{\beta}\int_0^t e^{-\beta(t-s)/2}\mathrm{d}W_s$.
The integral is Gaussian with variance
$\beta\int_0^t e^{-\beta(t-s)}\mathrm{d}s = 1 - e^{-\beta t}$, so

$$
X_t \mid X_0 \;\sim\; \mathcal N\!\big(e^{-\beta t/2}X_0,\;(1-e^{-\beta t})I\big),
$$

which is exactly $q(x_t\mid x_0) = \mathcal N(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,(1-\bar\alpha_t)I)$ with
$\bar\alpha_t = e^{-\beta t}$. No correction term appeared here because $f$ is linear in $x$;
it does appear as soon as you ask about $\|X_t\|^2$ (try Exercise 2).
</Example>

## Where it shows up

- The Fokker–Planck equation is Itô's lemma applied to $f(X_t)$ for a test function $f$,
  then taking expectations and integrating by parts. See
  [Fokker–Planck](/concepts/fokker-planck).
- Anderson's time-reversal formula, the basis of score-based sampling in
  <Cite id="2011.13456" />, is derived by applying Fokker–Planck forward and backward.
- The probability-flow ODE is what remains of the SDE when you move the diffusion term
  into the drift through Fokker–Planck.

## Exercises

<Exercise hint="Use Thm 1 with f(x) = x^2 and note the correction is constant.">
Show that $W_t^2 - t$ is a martingale by computing $\mathrm{d}(W_t^2)$.
</Exercise>

<Exercise hint="Vector form: f(x) = |x|^2, so the Hessian is 2I and the trace term is d·β.">
For the OU process above in $\mathbb{R}^d$, compute $\mathrm{d}\|X_t\|^2$ and find
$\mathbb{E}\|X_t\|^2$ as a function of $t$ and $\|X_0\|^2$.
</Exercise>

<Exercise hint="Apply Itô to f(x,t) = exp(θx − θ²t/2) and check the drift vanishes.">
Show that $M_t = \exp(\theta W_t - \tfrac12\theta^2 t)$ satisfies
$\mathrm{d}M_t = \theta M_t\,\mathrm{d}W_t$ and is therefore a martingale. This is the
building block of Girsanov's theorem.
</Exercise>
