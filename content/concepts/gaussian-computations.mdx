## Why you need this

Diffusion and flow models are built so that every intermediate distribution given the data is
Gaussian, and the whole derivation of DDPM (Ho et al. 2020) is a sequence of Gaussian
conditionings and Gaussian KLs: the posterior $q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)$ in their eq. (6)–(7)
is a conditional of a jointly Gaussian pair, and each ELBO term $L_{t-1}$ in eq. (8) is a KL
between two Gaussians. The denoising score matching target of Song et al. 2021,
$\nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t\mid x_0)$, is the score of a Gaussian. The conditional probability
paths of Lipman et al. 2022 are Gaussians with time-varying mean and scale. In robotics the
Kalman filter is Gaussian conditioning applied once per time step. This page makes each of
these a five-line computation.

## Setup

For $\mu\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and symmetric positive definite $\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$,

$$
\mathcal N(x;\mu,\Sigma) = (2\pi)^{-d/2}(\det\Sigma)^{-1/2}
\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12 (x-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\Big).
$$

We write $\Lambda = \Sigma^{-1}$ for the precision. The rules of
[matrix calculus](/concepts/matrix-calculus) for quadratic forms, $\log\det$, and the change of
variables are used throughout. We take as known the one-dimensional facts
$\int e^{-z^2/2}\mathrm{d}z = \sqrt{2\pi}$, $\mathbb{E}Z = 0$, $\mathbb{E}Z^2 = 1$ for
$Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$.

<Definition name="Multivariate normal">
$X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma)$ with $\Sigma\succ 0$ means $X$ has the density above. More
generally, for any $A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}$ and $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,I_m)$ (independent
standard normals), $X = \mu + AZ$ is called Gaussian with mean $\mu$ and covariance
$AA^\top$; this allows singular covariances, in which case there is no density on
$\mathbb{R}^d$. Prop 2 below shows the two definitions agree when $AA^\top\succ 0$.
</Definition>

## The idea

<Proposition name="Moment generating function" label="Prop 1">
If $X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma)$ (in either sense) then for every $a\in\mathbb{R}^d$
$$
\mathbb{E}\big[e^{a^\top X}\big] = \exp\!\Big(a^\top\mu + \tfrac12 a^\top\Sigma a\Big).
$$
</Proposition>

<Proof>
First $d = 1$, $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, $s\in\mathbb{R}$: complete the square,
$-\tfrac12 z^2 + sz = -\tfrac12(z-s)^2 + \tfrac12 s^2$, so
$\mathbb{E}[e^{sZ}] = e^{s^2/2}\int (2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-(z-s)^2/2}\mathrm{d}z = e^{s^2/2}$.
Now $X = \mu + AZ$ with $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,I_m)$: $a^\top X = a^\top\mu + \sum_j (A^\top a)_j Z_j$,
and the $Z_j$ are independent, so the expectation factors into
$e^{a^\top\mu}\prod_j e^{(A^\top a)_j^2/2} = \exp(a^\top\mu + \tfrac12\|A^\top a\|^2)$, and
$\|A^\top a\|^2 = a^\top AA^\top a = a^\top\Sigma a$. For the density definition, the same
completing-the-square computation in $d$ dimensions works with $\Sigma^{-1}$ in place of $1$.
$\blacksquare$
</Proof>

The MGF determines the distribution, so two Gaussians with the same $(\mu,\Sigma)$ are the
same distribution. That is the tool behind the next three results.

<Proposition name="Affine images and independence" label="Prop 2">
1. If $X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma)$ and $Y = BX + c$, then $Y\sim\mathcal N(B\mu + c, B\Sigma B^\top)$.
2. If $(X_1,X_2)$ is jointly Gaussian and $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_2) = 0$, then $X_1$ and
   $X_2$ are independent.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
(1) $\mathbb{E}[e^{a^\top Y}] = e^{a^\top c}\,\mathbb{E}[e^{(B^\top a)^\top X}]
= \exp(a^\top c + a^\top B\mu + \tfrac12 a^\top B\Sigma B^\top a)$, which is the MGF of
$\mathcal N(B\mu + c, B\Sigma B^\top)$.

(2) With block-diagonal $\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(\Sigma_{11},\Sigma_{22})$ and
$a = (a_1,a_2)$, the MGF is $\exp(a_1^\top\mu_1 + \tfrac12 a_1^\top\Sigma_{11}a_1)\cdot
\exp(a_2^\top\mu_2 + \tfrac12 a_2^\top\Sigma_{22}a_2)$, the product of the marginal MGFs. A
joint MGF that factors is the MGF of the product distribution. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Theorem name="Marginals and conditionals" label="Thm 3">
Partition $X = (X_1,X_2)\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma)$ with
$\mu = (\mu_1,\mu_2)$ and $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{11} & \Sigma_{12}\\ \Sigma_{21} & \Sigma_{22}\end{pmatrix}$,
$\Sigma_{22}\succ 0$. Then $X_2\sim\mathcal N(\mu_2,\Sigma_{22})$ and

$$
X_1\mid X_2 = x_2 \;\sim\; \mathcal N\!\Big(\mu_1 + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(x_2-\mu_2),\;
\Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}\Big).
$$

The conditional covariance $\Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}$ is the Schur
complement of $\Sigma_{22}$ in $\Sigma$; it does not depend on $x_2$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
The marginal is Prop 2(1) with $B = (0\ \ I)$. For the conditional, define the residual
$R = X_1 - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}X_2$. The pair $(R,X_2)$ is an affine image of $X$, so
jointly Gaussian, and

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(R,X_2) = \Sigma_{12} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{22} = 0 ,
$$

so by Prop 2(2) $R$ is independent of $X_2$. Its mean is $\mu_1 - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\mu_2$
and its covariance is

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(R) = \Sigma_{11} - 2\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}
+ \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{22}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}
= \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}.
$$

Now write $X_1 = R + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}X_2$. Conditioning on $X_2 = x_2$ fixes the
second term and leaves the distribution of $R$ unchanged (independence). So $X_1\mid X_2 = x_2$
is $R$ shifted by $\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}x_2$, which is Gaussian with the stated mean and
covariance. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Intuition>
$\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}$ is the regression coefficient of $X_1$ on $X_2$; for scalars it is
$\rho\sigma_1/\sigma_2$. The conditional variance $\sigma_1^2(1-\rho^2)$ is what is left after
the best linear predictor has been subtracted. For Gaussians the best linear predictor is the
best predictor, which is the content of the independence step in the proof.
</Intuition>

<Proposition name="Score and Hessian of a Gaussian" label="Prop 4">
For $p = \mathcal N(\cdot\,;\mu,\Sigma)$,
$$
\nabla_x\log p(x) = -\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu),\qquad \nabla^2_x\log p(x) = -\Sigma^{-1}.
$$
In particular for the DDPM forward kernel $q(x_t\mid x_0) = \mathcal N(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,(1-\bar\alpha_t)I)$,
$\nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t\mid x_0) = -\dfrac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0}{1-\bar\alpha_t} = -\dfrac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}$
where $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
$\log p(x) = -\tfrac12(x-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu) + \text{const}$; apply the gradient of a
quadratic form with symmetric matrix $\Sigma^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Proposition name="KL divergence between Gaussians" label="Prop 5">
For $p_0 = \mathcal N(\mu_0,\Sigma_0)$ and $p_1 = \mathcal N(\mu_1,\Sigma_1)$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$,
$$
\mathrm{KL}(p_0\,\|\,p_1) = \tfrac12\Big[\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_1^{-1}\Sigma_0)
+ (\mu_1-\mu_0)^\top\Sigma_1^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_0) - d + \log\frac{\det\Sigma_1}{\det\Sigma_0}\Big].
$$
</Proposition>

<Proof>
We need $\mathbb{E}_{p_0}[(X-m)^\top A(X-m)]$ for a fixed symmetric $A$ and vector $m$. Write
$X - m = (X-\mu_0) + (\mu_0 - m)$ and expand; the cross term has mean zero and
$\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu_0)^\top A(X-\mu_0)] = \mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(A(X-\mu_0)(X-\mu_0)^\top)
= \operatorname{tr}(A\Sigma_0)$. So

$$
\mathbb{E}_{p_0}[(X-m)^\top A(X-m)] = \operatorname{tr}(A\Sigma_0) + (\mu_0-m)^\top A(\mu_0-m).
$$

Now

$$
\log\frac{p_0}{p_1}(x) = \tfrac12\log\frac{\det\Sigma_1}{\det\Sigma_0}
- \tfrac12(x-\mu_0)^\top\Sigma_0^{-1}(x-\mu_0) + \tfrac12(x-\mu_1)^\top\Sigma_1^{-1}(x-\mu_1).
$$

Take $\mathbb{E}_{p_0}$: the second term gives $-\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_0^{-1}\Sigma_0) = -\tfrac d2$,
the third gives $\tfrac12[\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_1^{-1}\Sigma_0) + (\mu_0-\mu_1)^\top\Sigma_1^{-1}(\mu_0-\mu_1)]$.
$\blacksquare$
</Proof>

Sanity checks on Prop 5: it is zero when the two Gaussians coincide ($\operatorname{tr}I = d$,
$\log 1 = 0$); it is not symmetric; and for equal covariances it reduces to half the squared
Mahalanobis distance $\tfrac12\|\mu_1-\mu_0\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^2$, which is the form of every
$L_{t-1}$ term in DDPM.

<Theorem name="Isserlis / Wick formula" label="Thm 6">
Let $X\sim\mathcal N(0,\Sigma)$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$. Then for indices $i_1,\dots,i_{2n}$
(repetitions allowed)
$$
\mathbb{E}[X_{i_1}X_{i_2}\cdots X_{i_{2n}}] = \sum_{\text{pairings}}\ \prod_{(a,b)\in\text{pairing}}\Sigma_{i_ai_b},
$$
the sum running over the $(2n-1)!! = (2n-1)(2n-3)\cdots 1$ ways to split the $2n$ indices
into $n$ unordered pairs. Odd moments vanish. For $n = 2$:
$\mathbb{E}[X_iX_jX_kX_l] = \Sigma_{ij}\Sigma_{kl} + \Sigma_{ik}\Sigma_{jl} + \Sigma_{il}\Sigma_{jk}$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Sketch. Differentiate the MGF $M(a) = \exp(\tfrac12 a^\top\Sigma a)$: the moment is
$\partial_{a_{i_1}}\cdots\partial_{a_{i_{2n}}}M(a)\big|_{a=0}$. Each derivative either brings
down a factor $(\Sigma a)_{i}$ from the exponent or hits an existing factor $(\Sigma a)_{j}$
and turns it into the constant $\Sigma_{ij}$. At $a = 0$ every un-hit factor vanishes, so only
terms where the derivatives were consumed in pairs survive, one $\Sigma_{i_ai_b}$ per pair. The
bookkeeping of which derivative hit which factor is exactly an enumeration of pairings. The
one-dimensional case is the check: $M(s) = e^{s^2/2} = \sum_k s^{2k}/(2^k k!)$, so
$\mathbb{E}Z^{2n} = (2n)!/(2^n n!) = (2n-1)!!$, and $(2n-1)!!$ is the number of pairings of
$2n$ objects. For $n = 2$, $\mathbb{E}Z^4 = 3$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Warning>
Wick's formula needs mean zero. For $X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma)$ write $X = \mu + X^0$ and
expand; e.g. $\mathbb{E}[XX^\top] = \Sigma + \mu\mu^\top$ and
$\mathbb{E}\|X\|^4 = (\operatorname{tr}\Sigma)^2 + 2\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^2)$ only when $\mu = 0$.
</Warning>

## Worked example by hand

<Example title="Conditioning in two dimensions">
Let $(X_1,X_2)\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma)$ with $\mu = (1,2)^\top$ and
$\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}4 & 2\\ 2 & 3\end{pmatrix}$. Observe $X_2 = 4$. Thm 3 with scalars:

$$
\mathbb{E}[X_1\mid X_2 = 4] = 1 + \tfrac{2}{3}(4-2) = \tfrac73\approx 2.333,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(X_1\mid X_2 = 4) = 4 - \tfrac{2\cdot 2}{3} = \tfrac83\approx 2.667.
$$

Checks. The correlation is $\rho = 2/\sqrt{12} = 0.577$, and
$\sigma_1^2(1-\rho^2) = 4(1 - \tfrac13) = \tfrac83$ agrees. The conditional variance is
smaller than the marginal variance $4$, as it must be: observing $X_2$ can only reduce
uncertainty, and it reduced it by the fraction $\rho^2 = 1/3$. The conditional mean moved from
$1$ toward the direction of the surprise $x_2-\mu_2 = +2$ with slope $2/3$, less than $1$
because $X_2$ is noisier than its covariance with $X_1$. If we instead observed $X_2 = 2$, the
mean would stay at $\mu_1 = 1$. Good.
</Example>

<Example title="The DDPM posterior of the previous step given the current one and the data">
In one dimension, fix $x_0$ and let $\varepsilon,\varepsilon'\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ independent:

$$
x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\varepsilon,\qquad
x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon' ,
$$

with $\bar\alpha_t = \alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1}$. Given $x_0$, the pair $(x_{t-1},x_t)$ is an
affine image of $(\varepsilon,\varepsilon')$, hence jointly Gaussian, with

$$
\operatorname{Var}(x_{t-1}) = 1-\bar\alpha_{t-1},\quad
\operatorname{Cov}(x_{t-1},x_t) = \sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}),\quad
\operatorname{Var}(x_t) = \alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}) + (1-\alpha_t) = 1-\bar\alpha_t .
$$

Thm 3 with $X_1 = x_{t-1}$, $X_2 = x_t$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0]
= \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\big(x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0\big).
$$

Collect the $x_0$ coefficient: $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\big[1 - \tfrac{\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\big]
= \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\tfrac{1-\bar\alpha_t-\alpha_t+\bar\alpha_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}
= \tfrac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}(1-\alpha_t)}{1-\bar\alpha_t}$. So

$$
\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0) = \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}(1-\alpha_t)}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0
+ \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t,\qquad
\tilde\beta_t = (1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}) - \frac{\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})^2}{1-\bar\alpha_t}
= \frac{(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})(1-\alpha_t)}{1-\bar\alpha_t},
$$

which are exactly eq. (7) of <Cite id="2006.11239" />. With numbers $\alpha_t = 0.9$,
$\bar\alpha_{t-1} = 0.5$ (so $\bar\alpha_t = 0.45$): the $x_0$ coefficient is
$0.7071\cdot 0.1/0.55 = 0.1286$, the $x_t$ coefficient is $0.9487\cdot 0.5/0.55 = 0.8624$,
and $\tilde\beta_t = 0.5\cdot 0.1/0.55 = 0.0909$. Sanity: as $\alpha_t\to 1$ (no noise added
at step $t$) the $x_t$ coefficient tends to $1$, the $x_0$ coefficient to $0$, and
$\tilde\beta_t\to 0$: the posterior collapses onto $x_{t-1} = x_t$, as it should.
</Example>

<Example title="KL in one dimension, both ways">
$\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal N(0,1)\,\|\,\mathcal N(1,2)) = \tfrac12[\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 - 1 + \log 2] = \tfrac12\log 2 = 0.347$.
$\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal N(1,2)\,\|\,\mathcal N(0,1)) = \tfrac12[2 + 1 - 1 - \log 2] = 1 - 0.347 = 0.653$.
Not symmetric; the direction that puts the wide distribution first is larger because its
tails land where the narrow one has little mass.
</Example>

## Where it shows up

- DDPM, <Cite id="2006.11239" />: eq. (4) is the marginal $q(x_t\mid x_0)$ by iterating
  Prop 2(1); eq. (7) is the example above; eq. (8) is Prop 5 with equal isotropic covariances,
  giving $L_{t-1} = \frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\|\tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta\|^2 + \text{const}$.
- Score-SDE, <Cite id="2011.13456" />, eq. (7): the denoising score matching target is
  $\nabla_{x_t}\log p_{0t}(x_t\mid x_0)$, Prop 4 for the perturbation kernel; for the VE-SDE the
  kernel is $\mathcal N(x_0,(\sigma_t^2-\sigma_0^2)I)$, for the VP-SDE it is the DDPM kernel.
- Flow matching, <Cite id="2210.02747" />, Section 4.1: the conditional path
  $p_t(x\mid x_1) = \mathcal N(\mu_t(x_1),\sigma_t(x_1)^2 I)$ is pushed forward by the affine
  map $\psi_t(x) = \sigma_t x + \mu_t$, Prop 2(1), and Theorem 3 there is the unique vector
  field generating it. -
Kalman filtering: the update step is Thm 3 with $X_1$ the state and $X_2 = HX_1 + \text{noise}$
  the measurement; the Kalman gain is $\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}$. -
SAC's entropy bonus for a Gaussian policy is $\tfrac12\log\det(2\pi e\Sigma)$ (Exercise 1);
  the Gaussian [$W_2$ distance](/concepts/wasserstein-distance) and the Gaussian
  [Schrödinger bridge](/concepts/schrodinger-bridge) are built from the same objects.
- [Conditional expectation](/concepts/conditional-expectation) revisits Thm 3 as an
  $L^2$ projection, and [Stein's identity](/concepts/integration-by-parts-and-stein) is the
  integration-by-parts form of Prop 4.

<Exercise hint="Use the expected quadratic form computed in the proof of Prop 5 with A equal to the precision.">
Show that the differential entropy of $\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma)$ is
$-\mathbb{E}\log p(X) = \tfrac12\log\det(2\pi e\,\Sigma)$.
</Exercise>

<Exercise hint="Set X_1 = X and X_2 = Y in Thm 3; then compute the score of the marginal of Y with Prop 4 and compare.">
Let $X\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2)$ and $Y = X + \sigma\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$
independent. Compute $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y = y]$ from Thm 3 and verify Tweedie's formula
$\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y = y] = y + \sigma^2\nabla_y\log p_Y(y)$.
</Exercise>

<Exercise hint="There are 15 pairings of six indices; when all indices are equal each pairing contributes 1.">
Use Thm 6 to compute $\mathbb{E}Z^6$ for $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, and check it against the
coefficient of $s^6$ in $e^{s^2/2}$.
</Exercise>

<Exercise hint="Apply Prop 2(1) to the affine map that produces x_t from x_0 and noise, then Prop 5 with the isotropic DDPM prior.">
For the DDPM forward kernel with $\bar\alpha_T = 0.99$ in $d = 1$ and a data point $x_0 = 3$,
compute $\mathrm{KL}\big(q(x_T\mid x_0)\,\|\,\mathcal N(0,1)\big)$ and note how it depends on
$x_0$.
</Exercise>

## Going deeper

Bishop Section 2.3 derives the conditional and marginal by completing the square in the
precision matrix, the other standard route; the Schur complement identities used there are
collected in Rasmussen and Williams Appendix A.3. Petersen and Pedersen's Matrix Cookbook,
Section 8, lists every Gaussian identity on this page plus the ones for products of Gaussian
densities. For the degenerate case and characteristic functions, see any measure-theoretic
probability text (Durrett, Section 3.9).
